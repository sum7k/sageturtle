# Dawa: product case

For Gopalrao, 71, in `persona.md`.

## 1. The problem
"So many tablets, and they all look the same. Did I take the afternoon one? I do not want to trouble the children
over this."

## 2. Jobs to be done
- At the right time, tell me aloud which tablet, and show me what it looks like.
- Let me say "taken", and tell me if I ask again whether I already took it.
- If I miss one, let my son or daughter know without my having to say it.

## 3. What it is not for
- It does not advise on medicines, doses or illness. Only the doctor's slip decides.
- It does not sell or deliver medicines.
- Not a health record or a link to any hospital or insurer.

## 4. The number
**Doses taken on time**, as a share of doses due. Target: 9 in 10.

Guardrails that must not get worse:
- No double dose. If he says "taken" twice, the app stops him.
- At most one message a day to the family, and only for a missed dose. He can see what was sent.
- Setting up a new slip takes a family member under 5 minutes, from a photo.

## 5. The riskiest assumption
He will answer the phone's voice several times a day, every day, and not start to ignore it. Second risk: someone
in the family will set it up and keep it right when the doctor changes a dose.

## 6. Test with 5 real elders
- Who: 5 people over 65 who take 4 or more tablets a day, each with one family member who agrees to help. sumeet
  recruits them.
- How: no app needed. The family member sets phone alarms with a recorded voice ("Baba, the small white one, after
  food") and a photo of each strip on a card by the bed. Run for 10 days. Count the strips before and after.
- Pass: 4 of 5 take 9 in 10 doses by strip count, and none says the voice is a nuisance.
- If it fails: if they ignored the alarms, try a call from the family member's recorded voice instead of an alarm.
  If the family did not keep it up, the product is for the chemist or the doctor to set up, not the family.
