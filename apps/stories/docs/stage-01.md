# Stage 1 stories (draft for sumeet's review)

Written 2026-11-28 from the themes in themes.md. These are for listening: the learner cannot read yet. Each is
about two minutes when read aloud, in short sentences with lines that repeat so that a small child can join in.
Simple English is the source; the other languages come later.

## 1.1 The Shadow That Would Not Go Home

Mitti woke up early. The Sun was just coming up.

She stepped out of the cave, and there on the ground was a long, dark shape.

Mitti took a step. The shape took a step.

Mitti stopped. The shape stopped.

"Go home!" said Mitti. The shape said nothing. It did not go home.

Mitti ran. The shape ran too, right at her feet. She ran behind a rock. It bent itself over the rock and came along.

She found her friend Kanu. "Kanu, you take it," she said. But Kanu had a dark shape of his own.

Then Mitti saw something. When the Sun was on her left, the shape lay on her right. When she turned round, the shape jumped to the other side.

"It always runs away from the Sun!" said Mitti. "It is not following me. It is hiding from the light, behind me."

At midday the Sun was high above her head. The shape was tiny now, a little puddle under her feet. Mitti laughed. "Are you hiding under me?"

Then evening came. The Sun went down. Mitti looked at the ground.

No shape. Nothing at all.

"Oh," said Mitti. It was strange. She missed it.

That night Grandmother lit the fire in the cave. And there, on the cave wall, stood a giant dark Mitti, as tall as the roof!

Mitti was not afraid. She knew now. "Fire is a light too," she said. "Light in front of me, shadow behind me."

She lifted her hand. The giant lifted its hand. She put two fingers up, and on the wall a rabbit wiggled its ears.

Grandmother made a bird. Kanu made a deer.

And Mitti's shadow danced on the wall until the fire burned low.

## 1.2 You Cut, I Choose

Two sisters, Tara and Chhoti, went to the forest. Under a tree they found one big, sweet, yellow fruit.

One fruit. Two sisters.

"I will cut it," said Tara. She took a sharp stone and cut. She gave one piece to Chhoti.

Chhoti looked at her piece. She looked at Tara's piece. "Yours is bigger!" she cried.

The next day they found another fruit. "Today I will cut," said Chhoti. She cut, and she gave one piece to Tara.

Tara looked. "Yours is bigger!" she cried.

Every day it was the same. Cut, look, cry. The fruit was sweet, but nobody was happy.

One day Tara sat down and thought. She thought for a long time.

"Chhoti," she said, "here is a new way. One of us cuts. The other one chooses her piece first."

"I want to choose!" said Chhoti.

"Good. Then I cut," said Tara. She picked up the stone. Then she stopped.

"If I cut one piece big," she thought, "Chhoti will take the big piece. I will get the small one."

So Tara cut slowly. Very, very slowly. Right down the middle.

"Now choose," she said.

Chhoti looked at this piece. She looked at that piece. This one. That one. This one. That one.

"I cannot choose," she said at last. "They are just the same!"

Tara began to laugh. Chhoti began to laugh too. They ate their fruit, and it had never tasted so sweet.

The next day Chhoti cut and Tara chose. And do you know how Chhoti cut?

Slowly. Very, very slowly. Right down the middle.

## 1.3 The Bird With No Name

One morning a new bird came to the camp. It was small and green, with a red spot on its head. It sat on a high branch.

Nobody had seen a bird like it before.

"It is green like a leaf. I will call it Patta," said the big boy. "Patta! Patta! Come!"

The bird did not come.

"It has a red spot like a berry. I will call it Laali," said the big girl. "Laali! Laali! Come!"

The bird did not come.

"Chotu!" called one. "Hari!" called another. "Come! Come! Come!"

Everybody shouted a different name. The camp was full of noise. The bird hopped up to a higher branch.

Little Runu did not shout. She sat down under the tree. She sat very still. She listened.

For a long time there was only shouting. Then the others got tired and went away.

It was quiet now. And in the quiet, the bird sang.

"Tee-too. Tee-too."

Runu listened. "Is that your name?" she whispered. Then, very softly, she sang it back. "Tee-too."

The bird turned its head. It hopped down one branch.

"Tee-too," sang Runu.

Hop. One more branch. Hop. One more. And then the little green bird sat on a stone, right beside her.

It looked at Runu. It sang something new. "Ru-ru. Ru-ru."

It was looking at her when it sang it. Runu smiled a big smile.

"You have a name for me too," she said.

After that, every morning, a voice came from the tree. "Ru-ru!"

And a voice answered from the camp. "Tee-too!"

## 1.4 One Pebble for One Goat

Bholu was a little boy who looked after the goats.

Bholu could count: one, two, three. After three, he did not know. And there were many more goats than three.

"How will I know if all my goats come home?" he thought.

Bholu sat by the gate with a bag and a heap of pebbles.

In the morning the goats went out. One goat went out: one pebble in the bag. Plop. One more goat: one more pebble. Plop. One goat, one pebble. One goat, one pebble. Until all the goats were out and the bag was heavy.

All day the goats ate grass on the hill.

In the evening they came home. Now Bholu did it the other way. One goat came in: one pebble out of the bag. One goat, one pebble. One goat, one pebble.

The last goat came in. Bholu put his hand in the bag.

One pebble was still there.

"One pebble and no goat," said Bholu. "One goat is still out on the hill!"

It was getting dark. Bholu took the pebble in his hand and ran up the hill. He looked behind the rocks. He looked under the trees. Then he heard a small sound. "Meh-eh-eh."

There, behind a bush, was his brown goat. She was fine. Bholu was so happy.

"Come home," he said. "Here is your pebble." And he dropped it out of the bag.

But wait. Something small and wobbly stood up next to the brown goat. A baby goat, with wet ears and thin legs!

Bholu looked in his bag. Empty.

"One goat and no pebble!" He laughed and laughed.

He carried the baby home. And at the gate he picked up one new pebble, a small white one, just for her.

## 1.5 The Fire That Was Always Hungry

Grandfather made a small fire. It was warm and yellow. It went crackle, crackle.

"Children, look after the fire," said Grandfather. "I will be right here, mending my net."

The children sat around the fire. After a while it grew small. Crackle, crackle, it said, very softly.

"It is hungry," said the big boy. He gave it a stick. The fire ate the stick and grew.

"It is still hungry," said the big girl. She gave it two sticks. The fire ate them and grew more.

Crackle! Crackle! This was fun. They gave it a branch. They gave it dry leaves. They gave it a big log.

The fire grew and grew. Now it was taller than the children. It did not say crackle any more. It said ROAR. It was hot on their faces. Sparks flew up into the dark.

"Run!" shouted the big children, and they ran behind the rocks.

Only little Nanhi stayed where she was. She was holding one stick. She looked at the stick. She looked at the fire.

"When we give it food, it grows," she said. "So if we give it no food..."

She put her stick down on the ground, far from the fire.

"Don't feed it!" she called. "Nobody feed it!"

Nobody fed it. They stood and watched.

The fire ate the log. Then there was nothing left to eat. Slowly, slowly, the fire came down. Shorter than the big boy. Shorter than Nanhi. And then it was small and warm and yellow again. Crackle, crackle.

Grandfather had been watching all the time. He smiled.

"A fire eats only what you give it," he said. "Give it enough, and it is your friend. Who worked that out?"

Everybody pointed at Nanhi.

## 1.6 Who Took the Night?

Ullu was a baby owl. She loved the night. The night was cool and dark, and the stars were out.

But every time, the night went away. The sky turned bright, her eyes hurt, and she had to sleep.

"Somebody is taking my night," said Ullu. "I think it is the Sun. I will stay awake and catch the Sun."

Murga was a baby rooster. He loved the day. The day was warm and bright, and there were seeds to find.

But every time, the day went away. The sky turned dark, he could see nothing, and he had to sleep.

"Somebody is taking my day," said Murga. "I think it is the Night. I will stay awake and catch the Night."

So Ullu sat on her branch and kept her eyes open, open, open. And Murga sat on his wall and kept his eyes open, open, open.

Then the sky turned grey. Not dark, not bright. And for the first time, Ullu and Murga saw each other.

"Who are you?" said Ullu.

"Who are you?" said Murga. "Are you the one taking my day?"

"No! Are you the one taking my night?"

"No!"

They looked up. On one side of the sky, the last star was going. On the other side, the Sun was coming.

"Look," said Ullu slowly. "The night is not taken. It goes, and the day comes."

"And at evening the day goes, and the night comes," said Murga. "Nobody takes anything. They take turns!"

Under the tree hung a swing made of a vine. "Let us take turns too," said Murga. He had the swing all day. Ullu had the swing all night.

And every morning, when the sky was grey, they said hello.

## 1.7 The Hand on the Cave Wall

It rained and rained. The whole family sat inside the cave.

Little Rekha walked to the back of the cave, where the wall was smooth. And there she saw hands. Red hands, all over the wall! Many, many hands, pressed in red earth.

Rekha put her own hand on one of them. The red hand was much bigger.

"Too big," she said.

She tried another. "Too big." And another. "Too big!"

Her father came and put his hand on the biggest one. It fitted just right. "This one is mine," he said.

Her mother found hers. Her grandmother found hers, high up, a thin hand with long fingers. Her big brother found his.

"Everybody has a hand on the wall!" said Rekha. "Where is mine?"

She looked up. She looked along. Then she looked down, low on the wall, near the floor.

There was one tiny hand. So small, like a little leaf.

Rekha put her hand on it. Her hand covered it and went all the way past it.

"Too small," she said. "Whose is this? Nobody here is so small."

Her mother laughed softly. "That one is yours."

"Mine? But it is too small!"

"You were a baby when we made it. I held your hand on the wall, and you cried because the red earth was cold."

Rekha looked at the tiny hand. She looked at her own hand. "I was that small? And now I am this big?"

"You grew," said her mother. "Every day, a little."

Father mixed red earth with water. Rekha pressed her hand on the wall, right next to the tiny one. Now there were two hands. A baby one. And one from today.

"When I am big," said Rekha, "I will come and try again."

## 1.8 The Sweet That Looked Like a Stone

Grandmother put two things on a leaf.

One was a red berry. It was round and shiny, as bright as the evening Sun.

One was a brown lump. It was rough and dull. It looked like a stone.

"Choose one to eat," said Grandmother.

"The red one!" said Bittu at once. "It is so pretty." He put the berry in his mouth.

"Aaah! Thoo!" It was bitter! So bitter that his face went all crooked. He spat it out.

Grandmother put out her hand. She did not look at the leaf. Grandmother's eyes could not see any more. She picked up the brown lump and smelled it. She pressed it with her finger. She broke off a bit and touched it to her tongue. Then she smiled and gave it to Bittu.

Bittu was not sure. He licked it. It was sweet! Sweeter than fruit. It was honey, dried hard in the comb.

"Grandmother, how did you know? You cannot even see it!"

"My eyes do not help me," said Grandmother, "so I ask my other helpers. My nose. My fingers. My ears. My tongue, just a tiny bit. Your eyes told you only one thing: red is pretty. Pretty is not the same as sweet."

Now it became a game. The children brought her things in their closed hands.

She smelled. "Wet earth." She touched. "A feather." She listened to a shake. "Seeds in a dry pod."

Right, every time.

Then the children closed their own eyes and tried. They smelled. They touched. They listened.

"From now on," said Bittu, "I will look. And I will smell. And I will touch. And then I will choose."

## 1.9 The Longest Tail

One hot day the animals sat by the water, and they began to argue.

"I am the biggest," said the snake. "Look how long I am." He stretched out on the sand, from this rock to that rock.

"No, I am the biggest," said the giraffe. "Look how tall I am." She lifted her head above the trees.

"No, no. I am the biggest," said the elephant. "Look how heavy I am." He stepped on a log, and the log went crack.

"Long is biggest!" "Tall is biggest!" "Heavy is biggest!"

A little ant was listening. "Wait," said the ant. "You are all using different words. Let us try one word at a time. Who is the longest? Everybody lie down."

They all lay down on the sand. The snake was the longest, by far.

"Who is the tallest? Everybody stand up."

They all stood up. The giraffe was the tallest.

"Who is the heaviest? Everybody step on the log."

Only the elephant made it crack.

"So," said the ant. "The snake is the longest. The giraffe is the tallest. The elephant is the heaviest. Nobody is the biggest. Each one is just itself."

The animals were quiet. It was true.

"And what are you?" asked the snake kindly.

"I am the smallest," said the ant. "That is something too. I can go where none of you can."

Just then they all looked up. On the highest branch hung one ripe, golden fruit. The giraffe stretched her neck. Not enough. The elephant lifted his trunk. Not enough.

"Lift me," said the ant.

The elephant stood under the branch. The ant ran up his leg, up his back, up his trunk, onto the thin twig that could hold nobody else. Snip. The fruit fell down.

And there was enough for everyone.

## 1.10 The Clap That Crossed the River

Jhuri's family had a clap. It went like this: clap, clap... clap. Two quick, then a wait, then one more.

When somebody clapped the first part, clap, clap, somebody else finished it: clap. It meant, "I am here."

One morning Jhuri crossed the river on the stepping stones to pick berries. The berries were good. She picked and picked.

Then the fog came. White fog, thick like smoke. Jhuri could not see the river. She could not see the stones. She could not see her own feet.

"Mother!" she shouted. But the fog swallowed her voice.

Her heart went thump, thump. She wanted to run. But run where? The river was near, and she could not see it.

Jhuri stood still. She took a big breath.

"What do I know?" she thought. "I know our clap."

She clapped, loud and sharp. Clap, clap.

She listened. From her left came a sound: clap, clap.

Then from her right came another sound: clap.

Two answers! Which one was her family?

Jhuri thought. "The one on the left said what I said. Clap, clap. It only copies me. That is the big rock by the river. It sends every sound back."

"The one on the right did not copy. It finished the clap. Only my family knows how it ends."

She clapped again. Clap, clap. Left: clap, clap. Right: clap.

Jhuri turned to the right. She walked slowly, one small step at a time. Clap, clap. And the answer: clap. Nearer. Clap, clap. Clap. Nearer still.

Then a shape came out of the fog on the stepping stones, and two warm arms were around her.

"I heard you," said her mother. "You did not run. You clapped."

## 1.11 The Mountain You Could Put in Your Pocket

Far away from the camp stood a mountain.

Little Gogo looked at it. He held up a pebble next to it. The mountain was smaller than the pebble!

"What a tiny mountain," said Gogo. "I can jump over it. I will go and jump over it now."

His big sister Dhani smiled. "I will come with you," she said.

They walked. Gogo held up his pebble again. Now the mountain was as big as the pebble.

"It grew a little," said Gogo.

They walked more. Now the mountain was as big as his hand.

They walked and walked. Now it was as big as a tree. Then bigger than a tree. Then it was so big that Gogo had to bend his head all the way back to see the top. It filled up the whole sky.

Gogo stood at the bottom. He tried a little jump. He did not even get over its smallest stone.

"Dhani, who made it so big?"

"Nobody," said Dhani. "Did the mountain walk? Or did we walk?"

Gogo thought. "We walked."

"Now turn around," said Dhani.

Gogo turned around. Far, far away, he saw their camp. The big cave. The tall tree. The smoke from the fire. All of it was one small dot.

He held up his pebble. The whole camp was smaller than the pebble!

"My home is tiny! I could put it in my pocket!" Then he laughed. "No. My home is not tiny. I am far. Far things look small. Near things look big."

"And the mountain?" asked Dhani.

"The mountain was always big. I was far."

They walked home. With every step, the dot grew. A spot. A shape. A cave, a tree, a fire. And Mother, just the right size, waiting with food.

## 1.12 The Footprints Told

Mother had a pot of honey. She kept it in the hollow of the big tree.

One morning the pot was empty.

"Who ate the honey?" asked Mother.

Little Chiku stood there. His hands were sticky. His chin was sticky. Even his nose was sticky.

"The bear took it," said Chiku. "A very big bear."

"A bear?" said Mother. "Come. Let us look."

It had rained in the night, and the ground by the tree was soft mud. The mud was full of footprints.

Mother bent down. "Look at these. Are they big or small?"

"Small," said Chiku quietly.

"How many toes?"

Chiku counted. "Five."

"Put your foot in one."

Chiku put his foot in the footprint. It fitted just right. He looked at his foot. He looked at his sticky hands. His tummy felt heavy, and it was not from the honey.

"Mother," he said, "it was me. I ate the honey. There was no big bear."

Mother wiped his nose. "The mud already told me," she said. "But I am glad that you told me too. Do you feel better?"

Chiku did. The heavy feeling was gone.

"Now look once more," said Mother. "Look carefully."

Next to Chiku's footprints were other marks. Small and round, with tiny claw lines.

"These are not mine," said Chiku. "A baby bear was here! It came after me, to lick the pot."

"So the mud tells the truth both times," said Mother.

Some days later Chiku was playing by the tree, and he saw something brown moving in the bushes.

"Bear! Bear!" he shouted.

Nobody said, "Is it true?" Everybody came running at once, for Chiku was a boy who told what was so.

They all stood still and watched a mother bear and her cub walk slowly away, into the forest.
