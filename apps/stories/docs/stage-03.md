## 3.1 The Crack in the Seal

Kavi made beads. They were the best beads in the city. On every bag of beads she pressed her seal in wet clay. Her seal showed a little deer.

Kavi did not like her seal. Long ago she had dropped it, and now it had a thin crack. Every deer she printed had a line across its back leg. "It looks bad," she thought. But she had no time to make a new one.

One day a man came to her shop. He was angry. He threw a bag on the table. "Your beads broke in two days! Look, here is your deer."

Soon more people came. All had bad beads. All the bags had a deer seal.

"I did not make these," said Kavi. But who would believe her? The deer was hers.

Kavi looked at the angry man's bag for a long time. Then she took her seal and pressed it in fresh clay, next to his.

"Look at the two deer," she said. "Look at the back leg."

Her deer had a thin line across the leg. The deer on his bag had no line.

"Somebody has copied my seal," said Kavi. "But they copied a perfect deer. My seal is not perfect. It has a crack, and the crack prints every time."

The city guards looked for bags with a deer and no crack. In three days they found the man who made them.

Kavi never made a new seal. "That crack is the truest part of it," she said.

## 3.2 The Unknown Heap

An old merchant sat by a heap of dates. He had covered it with a cloth. Children stood all around him.

"Here is a riddle," he said. "A heap, and a quarter of the heap, make fifteen dates. How big is the heap?"

Nobody spoke. How can you count a heap you cannot see?

"I cannot know," said one boy, and he went away.

"I will wait until someone tells me," said one girl.

But Ila said, "I will guess. Let us say the heap is four."

"Four? Why four?"

"Because a quarter of four is easy. It is one. So four and one make five."

"Five is not fifteen!" The children laughed. "Your guess is wrong."

"Yes, it is wrong," said Ila. She was not sad at all. "But look how it is wrong. I got five. I need fifteen. Fifteen is three times five. So my guess is three times too small. Three times four is twelve. The heap is twelve."

"Check it," said the merchant, and his eyes were bright.

"A quarter of twelve is three. Twelve and three make fifteen." Ila looked up. "It is twelve!"

The merchant pulled off the cloth. The children counted. Twelve dates.

"The boy would not guess, so he learned nothing," said the merchant. "The girl waited, so she learned nothing. Ila made a wrong guess, and she looked at it closely. A wrong guess is not a shame. It is a ladder."

He gave the dates to all of them. Twelve dates, and how many children? That was the next riddle.

## 3.3 The Shopkeeper With Two Sets of Weights

In the market there was a shopkeeper named Dhaman. He bought grain from farmers and sold grain to the city people. And he was getting rich very fast.

Nobody knew his secret. Dhaman had two sets of weights. They looked just the same. But one set was a little heavy and one set was a little light. When he bought, he used the heavy ones, so he got more grain. When he sold, he used the light ones, so he gave less.

A girl named Rupa felt that her bag of grain was small. But how could she show it? She had no weights of her own.

She thought about it all night. In the morning she went to the shop. Many people were there.

"One measure of grain, please," she said. Dhaman put a weight on one pan and poured grain on the other until the pans were level. Rupa paid.

She walked to the door. Then she turned round. "I have changed my mind. I will sell you grain today. Please weigh this."

Dhaman did not think. He put grain on one pan and, as always when buying, took a weight from the other box. The pan with the grain went up.

"It is less than one measure," he said.

The shop went very quiet.

"A moment ago you told me this was one full measure," said Rupa. "Now you tell me it is less. The grain has not changed. So what has?"

A farmer took the two weights and put them on the two pans. One pan went down.

That day Dhaman threw one set of weights in the river. People still say, "Buy and sell with the same stone."

## 3.4 The Girl Who Wrote Herself a Door

Far away there was a city of scribes. In that city, anything written on clay and baked in the fire came true. But it came true just as it was written. Not as you wanted. As you wrote.

A girl named Lipi had just learned to write. It rained on her way to school and she got wet. So she wrote on a clay tablet, "No more rain," and she baked it.

The rain stopped. Good! But it did not come back. Day after day, no rain. The wells went low. The fields went yellow.

"Rub it out!" said her brother.

"I cannot. It is baked. Baked clay does not change."

Lipi thought hard. She could not take her words back. But she could add new words. She wrote, "Rain falls when the fields are dry," and she baked it.

That night the rain came, soft and long. Lipi was proud. Now she felt clever. She wrote, "I am the tallest in my class."

In the morning she was not one bit taller. But all the other children were as small as cats.

"Oh no. I did not say how," said Lipi. "I only said what."

She wrote quickly, "Everyone in my class is the height they were yesterday." Pop, pop, pop. The children grew back.

After that, Lipi read every sentence three times before she baked it. She asked, "What do these words say? Not what do I want. What do they say?"

Her teacher saw her doing it and smiled. "Now you can truly write," she said.

## 3.5 Half of a Half of a Half

A giant lived on the hill above the city. He was not a bad giant. But he was always afraid that he would have nothing.

The city baked a great tray of sweets and made him a promise. "Today you get half of the sweets. Tomorrow you get half of what is left. The next day, half of what is left. And so on, every day."

The first day the giant got a huge heap. He was happy.

The second day he got half of the rest. A good heap, but smaller.

The third day, smaller still. The giant began to worry. "Every day it is less. Soon it will be all gone. Then I will have nothing!"

A small girl named Adha brought him his share each day. "It will not be all gone," she said. "You only ever take half. So there is always the other half left."

"Always?"

"Think. If you take half of something, is something left?"

"Yes."

"And if you take half of that?"

"Something is left. But it is so small!"

"Small is not nothing," said Adha.

Days went by. The giant's share was one sweet. Then half a sweet. Then a crumb. Then a crumb of a crumb, so small that he could not pick it up. A line of ants came and carried it off.

The giant watched the ants. Then he began to laugh, deep in his chest.

"All this time I was given half," he said. "And the one who gave was never left with nothing."

The next day the giant came down the hill with his own basket of fruit. He gave half to the city. He still does.

## 3.6 The Shadow Clock Thief

The city had a clock, but it had no wheels and no bell. It was a tall stick in the market square. The shadow of the stick moved over the stones. When the shadow touched the red stone, the market closed.

One week, strange things happened. The market closed, but the Sun was still high. The next day it closed even sooner. The sellers were angry. "We are losing half our day!"

"Is the Sun going faster?" people asked.

A girl named Chhaya did not think so. Her name means shadow, and she knew shadows well. Every day, when the market closed, her own shadow was as long as her arm. Today the market closed, and she looked down. Her shadow was only as long as her hand.

"My shadow says it is early. The stick says it is late. The Sun is the same for both of us. So something is wrong with the stick."

She went close and looked. The stick was not straight up. Somebody had pushed it to lean over, so its shadow reached the red stone too soon.

That night she hid in the square. A small boy came with a water pot on his head. He put it down and pushed the stick a little more.

It was Panu, the water boy. He did not run. He just sat down, very tired. "I carry water from morning until the market closes," he said. "I only wanted the day to end."

Chhaya told the city what she had found, and she told them why.

The city set the stick straight again. And it painted a new stone, a blue one. When the shadow is shortest and touches the blue stone, everybody rests.

## 3.7 The Bridge Law

A law was cut into the wall beside the city bridge: NO ONE MAY CROSS THE BRIDGE AFTER DARK.

It was a good law. Long ago, people had fallen from the bridge at night. So a guard stood there every evening, and nobody crossed.

One night an old woman came running with a bag. She was a healer. "Let me pass! A child across the river is very sick."

The guard's name was Atal. He was a kind man, and he was an honest man. "I cannot. The law says no one may cross."

"The child may not live until morning."

Atal looked at the words on the wall. He looked at the healer. His head hurt.

"If I let her cross, I break the law," he thought. "If I stop her, a child may die. But why was this law made? So that people would not come to harm. If I keep the words tonight, a child comes to harm. Then I keep the words and break the reason."

Then he had an idea. He picked up his lamp. He picked up the healer too, bag and all, and put her on his back.

"What are you doing?" she cried.

"The law says you may not cross. You are not crossing. I am crossing, and I am the guard. You are only sitting."

He carried her over. The child lived.

In the morning Atal went to the judges and told them everything. "Punish me if you must. But please, mend the law."

They did not punish him. They cut new words into the wall: NO ONE MAY CROSS AFTER DARK, EXCEPT TO SAVE A LIFE, AND THEN WITH THE GUARD AND A LAMP.

## 3.8 The Buffalo on the Road

There was only one road into the city. One morning a buffalo lay down in the middle of it and went to sleep. It was the biggest buffalo anyone had ever seen.

Carts stopped. Soon there was a long line of carts.

People shouted. The buffalo slept. People clapped. The buffalo slept.

Then came Bhima, the strongest man in the city. He pushed with both hands. He pushed with his back. His face went red. The buffalo did not move even a finger's width.

"If Bhima cannot do it, nobody can," said the people.

A girl named Tula was watching. She had seen builders lift great stones with a long pole. She found a long, strong pole and a round stone.

She put the stone on the ground close to the buffalo. She put one end of the pole under the buffalo's side, over the stone. Then she went to the far end of the pole and pushed down with all her weight.

The buffalo's side went up! Just a little. Just enough to tip it awake.

"Mooo?" The buffalo opened its eyes and stood up.

"How did you do that?" asked Bhima.

"A long pole gives a small push a long arm," said Tula. "You pushed hard in a hard place. I pushed a little in a good place."

But the buffalo still stood in the road. Now it was awake and it did not want to go.

Tula did not pick up the pole again. She picked a handful of sweet green grass and held it out, and walked backwards.

The buffalo followed her off the road, eating happily.

"First the mind," said Tula. "Then be kind."

## 3.9 The Map With a Hole in It

Three children were digging in their yard. They found a flat piece of baked clay with lines on it.

"It is a map!" said Nakul. There was a wavy line for a river. There was a road. There were small squares for houses. And near the middle was a mark like a star.

"A star means treasure," said Bela.

But the map was broken. One corner was gone. The road ran to the broken edge and stopped. The star was past the edge, and only one point of it was left.

"We cannot find it now," said Nakul.

"Wait," said little Disha. "What do we know? A road does not just stop. It goes on the same way. And a river does not stop. It bends, but it goes on."

She put the map on the ground. With a stick she drew the road onward in the dust, straight. She drew the river onward. "The star is by the road, before the river. So it is about here."

They went to the real river and found an old road of flat stones. They walked along it, counting houses like the squares on the map. One, two, three, four.

The road came to a gate. It was their own gate.

"The map is of our own street!" said Bela. "From long, long ago."

The star was in their own yard, under the old fig tree. They dug. They did not find gold. They found a ring of old bricks, going down and down. At the bottom was cool, clean water.

It was a well, lost for hundreds of years. In the dry months that followed, the whole street drank from it.

## 3.10 The Debt and the Seed

In spring a girl named Beeja had no grain. She went to a rich farmer.

"Lend me one bowl of grain," she said. "At harvest I will give you two."

"Two is fair," said the farmer. "But if you are late, the debt doubles each moon. Do you promise?"

Beeja did not stop to think. "I promise."

She did not eat the grain. She planted it, every seed.

Harvest time came, but her plants were still green. She was late. Now she owed four bowls.

One more moon. The ears were yellow but not dry. Now she owed eight bowls.

One more moon. Rain came on the day she wanted to cut. Now she owed sixteen bowls!

Beeja sat and counted on her fingers. Two, four, eight, sixteen. "It grows so fast," she whispered. "One more moon and it is thirty-two. I gave my word too quickly. I did not see where doubling goes."

The next day the Sun came out. Beeja cut her field. She beat the grain and filled bowl after bowl.

Ten bowls. Twenty. Thirty bowls of grain, from the one bowl she had planted.

She carried sixteen bowls to the farmer. He was surprised. "I thought you would never pay."

"Your debt doubled three times," said Beeja. "But a seed in the ground does better than double. One grain gave me thirty."

She had fourteen bowls left. She ate some, and she kept the best for seed.

Next spring a small boy came to her door with an empty bowl. Beeja filled it. "Give me back one bowl at harvest," she said. "And if you are late, come and tell me. We will work it out."

## 3.11 Copper, Tin and the Bell

In the smith's shop lay three things: a lump of Copper, a lump of Tin and a grey Stone.

Copper was bright and red, but soft. You could bend him with your hands.

Tin was white and pretty, but weak. A knife of tin could not cut bread.

"Look at you two," said Stone. "Soft and weak. I am hard. People have made axes from me since the world was young."

Copper felt small. Tin felt small.

That evening the smith made his fire very hot. He put Copper in the pot. Copper became a shining pool. Then he dropped in a little Tin.

"I am sorry I am so weak," said Tin.

"I am sorry I am so soft," said Copper.

They mixed. The smith poured them out and let them cool.

What came out was not copper and it was not tin. It was a new thing, golden brown. Its name was bronze. The smith hit it with his hammer. It did not bend. He made a blade from it. It cut wood all day and stayed sharp.

"Where did that strength come from?" asked Stone. "Neither of you had it."

"We do not know," said the bronze. "It was not in him and it was not in me. It was in us together."

The smith made the rest of the bronze into a bell and hung it in the square. He hit it once. Dongggg. The sound flew over every roof. Copper alone had only gone clunk. Tin alone had only gone tick.

One night the river began to rise while the city slept. The watchman ran to the bell.

Dongggg. Dongggg. Every house woke up. Everyone was safe on the hill before the water came.

## 3.12 The Broken Pot Detectives

Outside the city was a low hill where nobody lived. One day three friends went digging there for fun.

Moti found pieces of a broken pot, black on the inside. Sona found black grains, as hard as little stones. Dhruv found a row of round holes in the ground, each one full of dark, soft earth.

"Just rubbish," said Moti.

"No. They are clues," said Sona. "Somebody lived here. Let us think like detectives. What do we see, and what does it tell us?"

"The pot is black inside. So it sat on a fire. They cooked here."

"These grains are wheat, but burnt. Nobody burns good food by choice. So there was a big fire, and they could not save the grain."

"And the holes?" asked Dhruv. "They are in a line, and then the line turns."

"Posts! Wood posts stood in them. The wood is gone, but its place is still here. This was a house. Look, we can walk around the walls."

They walked along the line of holes. It was a small house with one door.

"A family lived here," said Sona softly. "They cooked. They kept wheat. Then one day there was a fire, and they left."

Then Dhruv saw something small in the earth by the door. He lifted it out and wiped it. It was a tiny cart of baked clay, with two wheels that still turned.

Nobody spoke for a while.

"A child lived here," said Moti. "A child like us."

They put the cart back where it had been. Then Moti took a clay marble from his own pocket. He dug a small hole and put the marble in.

"For the next detective," he said. "So that they know we were here too."
