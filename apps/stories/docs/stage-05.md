## 5.1 The Iron Bowl That Would Not Drown

Loha was the smith's daughter. She had grown up among hammers, tongs and anvils, and she knew one thing that everybody knew: iron sinks.

Drop a nail in the river, and it is gone. Drop a hammer, and you had better be a good diver. Wood floats, iron sinks. Her father said so. The boatmen said so. The whole village said so.

One day Loha was washing the family's big iron cooking pot at the river. It slipped from her hands. She shut her eyes and waited for the splash and the sinking.

There was no sinking. The pot sat on the water, rocking gently, like a fat black duck. It began to float away. She had to run along the bank to catch it.

She carried it home, thinking hard.

"Baba, does iron sink?"

"Always," said her father, without looking up.

"The pot did not."

"Then it is not all iron."

"You made it yourself. From the same bar as the hammer."

Her father put down his tongs. He had no answer, and he was honest enough to say so. "I do not know. Everyone knows iron sinks. I never asked about the pot."

"Everyone knows" was not good enough for Loha. She took the pot back to the river, and a lump of iron of the same weight, and she began to test.

The lump sank at once. The pot floated. Same iron. Same weight. Only the shape was different.

She pressed the empty pot down into the water with her hands. The water pushed back, hard. The deeper she pressed it, the harder the water pushed. "The pot is wide and hollow," she said. "To sit in the water, it must push a great deal of water out of the way. And the water pushes back up. The lump is small. It pushes only a little water aside, so it gets only a little push."

She put stones in the pot, one at a time. The pot sat lower and lower. With ten stones it still floated. With fifteen, the water was near the rim. With sixteen, water poured over the edge and the whole thing went down.

"So it is not the iron that floats or sinks," she told her father that night. "It is the iron and the shape together. A hollow shape that pushes aside enough water will carry its own weight, and a good load too. I counted. Fifteen stones."

Her father listened, and he rubbed his beard. But the neighbours laughed. "Floating iron! Next she will tell us that stones fly."

Loha did not argue. She had tested it, and they had not. She knew which of them was guessing.

Two months later the rains came early. They came hard, and for four days they did not stop. On the fifth morning the river came over its banks. The low end of the village went under water, and the smithy stood in the low end.

People carried out their pots and beds. But the smith stood in his doorway, with water round his knees, and he did not move.

His anvil. His hammers. His tongs, his chisels, his bars of iron. A lifetime of tools, and every one of them heavy. The only boat in the village was already full of children and old people. The water was rising.

"Leave them, Baba," said a neighbour. "Iron cannot swim."

Loha was already wading into the house. She came out pulling the great iron cauldron, the one her father used for quenching, big enough for a child to sit in. She set it on the water. It floated.

"Fifteen stones," she said. "This one is bigger. It will take more. Put the hammers in. Not all on one side. Spread them."

Her father stared at her. Then he began to load.

They used every iron vessel in the smithy: the cauldron, two cooking pots, a trough. Loha watched the water line on each one and called out when to stop. "No more in that one. The rim is three fingers above the water. Keep it so."

They tied the vessels in a line with rope, and they walked them through the brown water to the high ground, like a line of ducklings. Hammers, tongs, chisels and bars all rode along, dry.

The anvil was too heavy for any pot. It stayed in the mud until the water went down. It did not mind.

When the village was dry again, the neighbours came to the smithy one by one, a little shy.

"That thing about the shape," they said. "Show us."

Loha showed them, with a lump and a pot, at the river's edge.

"Iron sinks," she said. "And iron floats. It depends on what you make of it. Next time everybody knows something, I think I will still go and look."

## 5.2 The Town Behind the Mirror

Vaam did everything with his left hand. He ate with it, threw with it and wrote with it. In his town, this was thought to be wrong.

"Use your proper hand," said his teacher, and tapped his knuckles.

"Wrong-handed Vaam!" called the other boys. They did not mean much by it. But he heard it twenty times a day.

When Vaam wrote, his hand dragged across the wet ink and smudged it. So in secret he had found a trick. He wrote backwards, from the right side to the left, with every letter turned round. That way his hand moved away from the ink. It felt as easy as running downhill. Nobody else could read it. He called it his own writing.

At the edge of the town stood an old gateway with no wall around it. Inside the gateway hung a sheet of something bright, like still water standing on its side. People said it was a mirror from the old days. Nobody went near it.

One evening, after a bad day, Vaam walked up to it. He saw himself: a thin boy with ink on his fingers. He lifted his left hand. The boy in the mirror lifted the hand on the same side.

"You look as if you understand," said Vaam. He put his palm on the bright sheet, and the sheet was not there. He fell through.

He stood up in the same town. The same street, the same well, the same big tree. But the tree was on the other side of the road. The well was on the other side of the square. Everything was turned round.

A woman was drawing water. She turned the handle the other way. A man greeted his friend, and they shook left hands. Over a shop hung a sign. Vaam read it at once. It was in his own writing! Backwards letters, right to left. Here it was just writing.

A girl of his age stared at him. "You came out of the gate! Nobody comes out of the gate. Are you from the other side? Is it true that the people there are all the wrong way round?"

Vaam began to laugh. He laughed so hard that he had to sit down. "They say that about you," he said.

Her name was Dakshi. She showed him the school. All the children wrote with their left hands, and one boy was having his knuckles tapped. "He will use his right hand," she said. "We call him wrong-handed. Is that not sad? He cannot help it."

Vaam looked at that boy for a long time.

"He is not wrong," he said. "And I am not wrong. In my town I am him. Here I am everybody else. I did not change when I stepped through. Only the side changed."

"But one side must be the right way round," said Dakshi.

"Which? Stand facing me. Lift the hand on the side of the tree." They both did. He lifted his left and she lifted her right. "Same side. Different names. If you turn round, it swaps. The hand does not know what it is called."

Dakshi tried it three times. Then she said slowly, "So nobody is the wrong way round. Each of us is just one way round."

The grown-ups took longer. The elders of the mirror town came to look at Vaam. The elders of his own town came to the gateway and looked through, once they saw he had come to no harm. The two sides stood and stared at each other through the gate.

They could not talk business at first. Each side's letters were scribbles to the other. Each side's handshake missed, a right hand reaching for a left, like two people trying to pass in a narrow lane.

Only one person could read both. Only one person could shake a hand on either side without thinking.

So Vaam sat in the gateway with a table. A weaver from his town held up a price list. Vaam wrote it out backwards for the mirror town. A potter from the mirror town wrote an order. Vaam read it aloud. Cloth went one way. Pots came the other. And it turned out that the mirror town had no good salt, and Vaam's town had no good honey.

Dakshi sat beside him and learned the other writing. The boy with the tapped knuckles learned it fastest of all. For him it ran downhill.

Vaam's teacher came to the gateway one day. He watched Vaam write a letter with his left hand, right to left, quick and clean.

"I told you that was your wrong hand," said the teacher. "I am sorry. It was only your other one."

## 5.3 The Debt Below Nothing

Seth Ginti was a merchant, and he loved his counting board. Stones in rows: ones, tens, hundreds. He could tell you how much grain, how much cloth and how many goats he had, to the last one.

"Counting is simple," he liked to say. "You have some, or you have none. Nobody can have less than nothing."

His daughter Rina kept the family's accounts on palm leaves. One evening she came to him with a worried face.

"Baba, how many goats do we have?"

"None at present. I sold the last one."

"And do you remember that we bought six goats from the potter in spring, for the feast, and said we would pay him back three goats after the harvest?"

"I remember."

"The harvest is over. So we have no goats. And we owe three. If someone gave us three goats tomorrow, would we have three goats?"

"No. They would go straight to the potter. We would have none."

"So we are three goats away from having none. We are below none. By three."

Seth Ginti opened his mouth. "Nobody can have less than..." He stopped. He looked at his counting board. There was no place on it for this.

"A hole in the ground," said Rina. "That is how I think of it. Flat ground is nothing. A heap of earth is something. A hole is less than nothing. You must fill it before you are back at flat. Our goats are a hole three deep."

She had begun to write such numbers on her leaves with a small mark beside them, a mark for "owed". Three owed. Five owed. Her father looked through the leaves, and he grew more and more unhappy.

"The potter is angry with me," he said. "He has asked for his goats twice. I cannot pay, because the weaver owes me three goats' worth for cloth and will not pay. I have not spoken to the weaver for a year. And the weaver says he cannot pay, because somebody owes him. The whole market is cross with itself."

"Who owes the weaver?"

"How should I know?"

Rina went to find out. She was a child, so people talked to her. She took a leaf and a sharp stick.

The weaver was sour. "I would pay your father tomorrow. But the potter owes me three goats' worth for the cloth of his daughter's wedding. He says he cannot pay until he is paid. A year I have waited!"

Rina wrote it down. Then she sat under the market tree and looked at her leaf for a long time.

She drew three dots: Merchant, Potter, Weaver. She drew an arrow for each debt.

Merchant owes Potter three. Potter owes Weaver three. Weaver owes Merchant three.

The arrows went round in a ring.

She gave each of them a number, her new way. Her father: three owed to him, three owed by him. A heap of three and a hole of three. She put the heap in the hole. Flat ground.

The potter: a heap of three, a hole of three. Flat.

The weaver: flat.

She ran home. "Baba! Call the potter and the weaver. Nobody needs to pay anything."

The three men stood in the yard and would not look at each other. Rina drew the ring in the dust.

"Suppose I had three goats, and I lent them to Baba for one moment," she said. "Baba gives them to the potter. His debt is paid. The potter gives them to the weaver. His debt is paid. The weaver gives them to Baba. His debt is paid. Baba gives me back my three goats. Everybody has paid everybody. And the goats are back where they began."

"So?" said the potter.

"So the goats did nothing but walk in a circle. We do not need them. Each of you is owed three and owes three. That comes to nothing. Not less than nothing, and not more. Just say, all three of you, 'It is done.'"

There was a long silence. The potter counted on his fingers. The weaver drew the arrows again himself, in case the child had tricked him.

"A year," said the weaver. "A year we have not spoken. Over three goats that nobody had and nobody needed."

"It is done," said the potter.

"It is done," said the weaver.

"It is done," said Seth Ginti. Then he sent for sweets, and those he paid for.

That night he made a new row on his counting board, on the left side, with dark stones. "For holes," he said.

"Can you have less than nothing, Baba?" asked Rina.

"You can," said her father. "I had it for a year, and I did not know its name. It is easier to fill a hole when you can count how deep it is."

## 5.4 The Game Where Nobody Lost

Prince Ajeya was eleven, and he had a board game with four kinds of pieces: elephants, horses, chariots and foot soldiers. The game was called chaturanga. He played it every day against the old men of the court.

He played in one way. He sent his foot soldiers forward first, all eight of them, and let them be taken. While the other player was busy taking them, his elephants and chariots broke through. He usually ended the game with one or two pieces standing on a board swept bare.

"They are only foot soldiers," he said. "That is what they are for."

In the palace lived an old soldier named Sipahi, who had one stiff leg and looked after the armoury. One day the prince asked him for a game.

Sipahi set up the pieces. Then he said, "Before we begin, my prince, will you do one thing for me? Give each of your foot soldiers a name."

"A name? They are bits of wood."

"Humour an old man."

The prince shrugged. He touched them one by one. He ran out of ideas at once, so he used the names of the boys who looked after his horses. "Ramu. Gopal. Little Bansi. Kesar. Dev..." Eight names.

They played. On the third move, as always, the prince pushed a foot soldier forward to be taken.

Sipahi picked it up. "That was Little Bansi," he said, and he put it gently in the box.

The prince moved another. "Gopal," said Sipahi. Into the box.

By the fifth, the prince's hand was slower. By the seventh, he sat looking at the board for a long time without moving.

"It is only a game," he said.

"It is," said Sipahi. "I was a foot soldier for thirty years. I know that it is a game for the one who moves the pieces."

The prince finished. He ended with a chariot and an elephant on an empty board, as usual. But it did not taste as it usually did. He kept looking at the box.

"You are thinking that I am soft," said Sipahi. "I am not telling you to be soft. I am telling you to count. You count what you take. You do not count what you spend. A merchant who did that would be poor in a year."

The next day a girl was in the armoury, oiling the leather. She was Sipahi's granddaughter, Raksha. The prince, still out of temper, said, "Do you play?"

"A little."

He played his old way. He pushed his soldiers forward to be taken.

She did not take them.

She moved her own pieces quietly so that each one stood guard over another. His soldier stood right in front of hers, offered. She left it there and made her line stronger. He sent a horse. There was no gap for it. He sent a chariot. It could go nowhere without being lost for nothing.

He needed her to take his pieces. His whole plan was built on her being greedy. She was not. After forty moves, all of his pieces and all of hers were still on the board, and he could not move forward one square.

"Why do you not take them?" he burst out.

"Why should I? You are giving them away to make me leave my place. I like my place." She looked up. "And you named them. Grandfather told me. It is hard to take a piece called Little Bansi."

The prince sat back. Nobody had won. Nobody had lost. He had never seen a board so full at the end of a game. It looked like two armies that had decided to go home for dinner.

"This is dull," he said. But it was not dull. He had thought harder in that game than in a hundred others.

"Then let us make it less dull," said Raksha. "A new game. Same board. Same pieces. But we play on one side, you and I. The board is the puzzle. We must bring all sixteen foot soldiers, yours and mine, across to the far rows. If even one is left behind or blocked for good, we both have failed."

They tried. It was very hard. The pieces got in each other's way. Twice they jammed the whole board. On the fourth evening they did it: sixteen foot soldiers across, with elephants and horses making way.

The prince found that he was grinning.

He kept playing chaturanga in the ordinary way too. But he played it differently. When he gave up a piece now, it was because he had counted what it would cost and what it would bring. More often than not, the count told him to keep it.

Years later, when he was king, people said of him that he was slow to go to war and that his soldiers would follow him anywhere. He kept one small wooden foot soldier on his table. If anyone asked, he said, "That is Little Bansi. He reminds me to count."

## 5.5 Proof, Please

The town of Sabit had one custom that travellers found strange. Nothing was believed there without proof.

If you said, "It will rain tomorrow," someone would say, "Proof, please." If a shopkeeper said, "Best mangoes in the land," a child would say, "Proof, please," and expect a slice. It made the people of Sabit hard to cheat. It also made them, perhaps, a little proud.

One evening a stranger walked into the square. He wore a fine coat and had a voice like warm oil.

"People of Sabit! I hear you believe only what is proved. Good. I shall prove to you that every one of you owes me a dinner."

The crowd laughed. "Proof, please!"

"Gladly. I shall go one step at a time, and you shall stop me if a step is false. First: if a person gives you something of value, you owe that person something in return. Is that not just? If the baker gives you bread, you owe the baker."

People nodded. That was how the market worked.

"Second: a thing may have value even if you cannot hold it. A song has value. Good advice has value. True?"

"True."

"Third: I am a traveller from far away. I have brought you my greeting, my news of the road, and my company this fine evening. These have value. You are listening, are you not? Nobody listens to what has no value."

Some people frowned, but they were, in fact, listening.

"Fourth: who knows the worth of a thing better than the one who made it? The baker sets the price of bread. So I set the price of my greeting. And I set it at one dinner. Each."

He bowed. "So. You have received something of value. You owe something in return. The price is one dinner. Every step was agreed. You are people of proof. I shall begin with that house there, the one with the smell of fried onions."

The square was silent. People went back over the steps. Each one had sounded right. And yet the end was plainly silly. The grown-ups began to argue with each other in low voices. Nobody wanted to be the first to say, "I agreed to a step that I should not have."

Three children sat on the well: Tark, his sister Yukti, and their small friend Pramaan.

"Something is wrong in it," said Tark. "I cannot see which step."

"When the answer is silly, a step is bad," said Yukti. "That is certain. A chain of true steps cannot end in a false thing."

"Then we must find the bad link."

"That is one way," said Yukti slowly. "Here is another. If his steps are good, they are good for everyone. Not only for him. Let us use them."

She stood up on the well. "Sir! We accept your proof."

The grown-ups gasped. The stranger smiled, a little too soon.

"Step one: if a person gives you something of value, you owe them in return. Step two: a thing can have value though you cannot hold it. Now, step three. For this whole hour, we have given you our listening. Two hundred people, standing still. A speaker with no listeners has nothing. You came here for our listening. So it has value to you."

The stranger's smile grew thin.

"Step four: who knows the worth of a thing better than the one who gives it? You said so. So we set the price of our listening. Pramaan, what is the price?"

"Breakfast," said Pramaan. "For a year."

"Each," said Tark.

"So you owe two hundred people breakfast for a year," said Yukti. "Every step was yours. You may begin tomorrow. We like puris."

For a moment the stranger stood quite still. Then he threw back his head and laughed, a real laugh, not the oily one.

"Caught in my own net! Very well. I shall pay my debt in another coin. I shall show you the bad link myself." He held up four fingers and bent one down. "It is the fourth. The baker may name his price, yes. But you may say no and walk away. A price is only a price when the other side has agreed to it before the giving. I gave you a greeting that nobody asked for, and then I named the price afterwards, alone. That is not trade. That is a trap dressed as trade."

"Why did you do it?" asked Tark.

"I go from town to town. In most of them, people cannot find the bad step, and they are ashamed to say so. So somebody feeds me, just to end it." He looked at Yukti. "You did not find the bad step either."

"No," said Yukti honestly. "I only knew that there must be one. If a proof works, it works for anyone who uses it. Yours gave a silly answer for you and a sillier answer for us. So it was broken. Finding where came second."

"That is a good tool," said the stranger. "Keep it."

The woman from the house with the fried onions gave him dinner after all. Nobody owed it. She said she just felt like it. And that needs no proof.

## 5.6 The Well at Noon

Dhoop lived in a town in the south. Her friend Chhaon had moved away the year before, to a great city far to the north. It was fifty days by camel. They wrote to each other by every caravan.

In the middle of summer Dhoop wrote:

"Something strange happens here once a year, on the longest day. At noon the Sun shines straight down our deep well. You can see it shining in the water at the bottom. And for a moment nothing has a shadow. Not the trees, not the pillars, not I. Does it happen in your city?"

The answer came three months later.

"You are making fun of me. I went out on the longest day at noon, as you said. I looked down our well. The bottom was dark. Everything had a shadow. Short, but there. The Sun does not stand straight overhead here, ever. I think you made it up, to seem interesting."

Dhoop was hurt. She wrote back hotly: "I do not make things up. You did not look properly."

A cold season passed with no letters at all.

Then Dhoop began to think. She was angry, but she was also honest, and she asked herself a question. "Chhaon has never lied to me. Not once in eight years. Which is more likely: that my oldest friend has turned into a liar, or that both of us saw truly and there is something that I do not understand?"

She wrote a different kind of letter.

"I am sorry for my last letter. I believe you. Please believe me. If we both saw what we saw, then the same Sun, at the same moment, was straight above me and not straight above you. That is a puzzle, not a quarrel. Let us measure it. Next longest day, at noon, stand a straight stick in the ground. Measure the stick and its shadow. I will do the same."

The reply came by the fastest caravan of the year. "I am sorry too. I have already cut the stick."

The longest day came. In the south, Dhoop stood her stick up. At noon it had no shadow at all.

In the north, Chhaon stood up a stick of the same length. At noon its shadow was short, but clear. He measured it with great care, three times.

When the letters crossed, each drew the other's stick beside their own.

Chhaon wrote first with an idea. "Suppose the Earth is flat, like a table. Then the Sun must be near, like a lamp above a table, for your stick and mine to make different shadows. But the Sun cannot be so near. It looks the same size from your town and from mine."

Dhoop wrote back: "Then suppose the Sun is very, very far, so that its light comes down the same way everywhere. Then on a flat Earth our sticks would make the same shadow. They do not. So the Earth is not flat. If the ground is curved, like the skin of a great ball, my stick and yours point different ways, though each stands straight up. Mine points at the Sun. Yours leans away from it a little. That lean is your shadow."

Chhaon's teacher was an old woman who studied the sky. He showed her the two drawings. She looked at them for a whole evening.

"Your stick leans away from your friend's by a small angle," she said. "I have measured it from your shadow. If you kept walking north, and at each town the sticks leaned a little more, in the end you would come all the way round. Your angle is one part in fifty of the full way round."

Chhaon wrote it in his biggest letters. "One part in fifty! The road from you to me is one fiftieth of the way round the whole Earth. And we know that road. It is fifty days by camel. So the whole Earth is fifty times fifty days by camel. Two thousand five hundred days round. Dhoop, we have measured the world. With two sticks and a well."

Dhoop read that letter under the tree in her yard. Then she read it again.

She wrote: "Do you see what nearly happened? We nearly stopped writing. I thought you were calling me a liar. You thought I was showing off. If either of us had gone on being cross, each of us would have kept one stick and one shadow, and one stick tells you nothing. It needed two. And it needed each of us to believe that the other one measured honestly."

The last line of Chhaon's answer is the one she kept all her life.

"When a friend tells you something you find impossible, and you know the friend is honest, do not throw away the friend or the fact. Keep both, and look for what is big enough to hold them together. This time it was the whole Earth."

## 5.7 The Painter Who Stole the Roundness

Chitra was a painter, and she had spent twenty years learning one thing: how light falls.

She had noticed that a ball in the sunlight is never one colour. On the side towards the Sun it is bright. As it turns away it grows darker, little by little. On the far side it is darkest, but with a soft glow at the very edge, where light comes back up from the ground. And under it lies a shadow.

She painted exactly that, on the flat wall of the palace hall: a red ball lying on a painted shelf.

Prince Chapal came running through the hall. He was nine. He saw a red ball on a shelf and reached out to take it.

His fingers hit the wall.

He tried again. Flat, cold wall. He ran his whole hand over it. There was nothing there but paint. But when he stepped back, there was the ball again, round and solid, ready to be picked up.

The prince shouted for the guards.

"She has put a spell on the wall! She has stolen a ball and locked its roundness inside the stone. She is a sorcerer!"

Chitra was brought before the king. People pressed in to see. Half of them had already poked the wall themselves and come away uneasy.

"What do you say, painter?" said the king. "My son says this is magic."

"It is not magic, my lord. It is light and shade. May I show how it is done?"

"A sorcerer would say just that," muttered someone.

"A sorcerer would hide how it is done," said Chitra. "I will hide nothing. Give me a bare piece of wall, some colour and the prince."

She drew a circle and filled it with flat red. "Is that a ball, prince?"

"No. That is a red plate. It is flat."

"Good eyes. Now, where is the window in this hall?"

"There. On the left."

"So light comes from the left. On a real ball, the left side would be bright." She brushed a paler red on the left. "And the right side, away from the window?" She laid a deep, dark red down the right. "Is it a ball now?"

The prince tilted his head. "It is... beginning to swell."

"Now the trick inside the trick. The dark side is not darkest at its very edge. A little light bounces up from the shelf." She put the thinnest rim of soft colour along the dark edge. Then, under the circle, a grey shadow lying to the right.

The crowd breathed out all together. The red plate had become a ball. It had happened in front of their eyes, in the time it takes to eat a banana.

"Touch it," said Chitra.

The prince touched it. Wet paint came off on his finger. He looked at his finger and then at the ball, and he began to laugh.

"My eye says round. My finger says flat. Which is lying?"

"Neither," said Chitra. "Your finger tells you what the wall is. Your eye tells you how the light is arranged. I arranged it as a ball would. Your eye has seen balls all your life, so it finishes the story by itself. I did not put roundness in the wall. You did."

"Then it is still a kind of trick," said the king slowly. "You make people see what is not there. How is that different from a cheat?"

Chitra had thought about this for twenty years.

"My lord, a storyteller says, 'Once there was a tiger who could talk.' Is he lying? No. He and his listeners both know it is a story. That is art. But if he comes to your gate and says, 'A tiger is loose, give me money to chase it,' and there is no tiger, that is a lie. The words may be the same. The difference is whether the other person knows. My ball sits on a wall, in a frame, in a hall of paintings, with my name under it. It says, 'I am a picture.' And I will show anyone how it is made. A trick that is used to take something from a person who does not know, that is the cheat."

The king nodded. "Then teach my son. He should know how his eyes can be led."

So the prince learned light and shade. He was not very good at it, but he learned to look.

There is one more part to the story. The palace treasury had a long outer wall, with one small door at the far end. The treasurer worried about thieves. Chitra offered to help.

In the middle of the long wall, far from the real door, she painted a doorway. It stood half open. Through it you could see a dim passage, a lamp, and the corner of a chest. She put a line of light on the door's edge and a shadow on the floor.

In daylight everybody knew it was a painting. Children came and bumped their noses on it for fun.

But one moonless night, two thieves crept along that wall. They saw a door standing open, and a lamp far inside. They could not believe their luck. They ran at it, the first thief with his head down.

The guards said afterwards that the sound was like someone dropping a melon.

The thieves were found sitting on the ground, holding their noses. One of them was still arguing with the wall.

"Was that not a cheat?" the prince asked Chitra. "They did not know it was a picture."

Chitra smiled. "A fair question. But think. It is on the outside of a treasury. Who is fooled by it? Only somebody creeping up in the dark to go where he has no right to go. It takes nothing from an honest person. And in the morning I will show them, too, how it was done. They will have time to study it."

## 5.8 The Dancer With Nine Faces

A band of players came to the village of Rangpur every year after harvest. They put up a stage of bamboo, and for seven nights they told stories with dance, song and their faces.

The youngest of them was a girl named Nava. She was ten. She could do something that made people forget to breathe.

Her teacher, the old dancer Guru Amma, had taught her that the players of old had counted the great moods that a person can feel. They had counted nine. Love. Laughter. Sorrow. Anger. Courage. Fear. Disgust. Wonder. And the ninth, which comes when the others have passed: peace.

"Each one lives in the body in its own way," Guru Amma said. "Anger pulls the brows down and makes the breath short and hot. Fear opens the eyes and pulls the shoulders up. Sorrow makes everything heavy, and the corners of the mouth cannot lift. Learn where each one lives. Then you can put it on, and take it off."

Nava learned. On stage she could move from one to the next like someone turning the pages of a book.

On the third night she played a queen whose son was lost. She did sorrow. The whole of Rangpur wept. A grandmother at the front climbed up on the stage with a cup of water. "Child, child, do not cry so. Who has hurt you?"

Nava stopped at once and smiled at her. "Nobody, Grandmother. I am well. It is the queen who is sad."

The grandmother stared. "But your tears are wet!"

"Yes. I let sorrow come into my face and my breath. And now I let it go." She breathed out, shook her hands, and her face was a ten-year-old's again.

The next night she played a warrior in a rage. A farmer in the crowd stood up, alarmed. "Somebody calm that girl before she hurts herself!"

The village talked of nothing else. "How can she be so angry and then, like that, not angry?" "When I am angry, I am angry until the next day." "When my husband is angry, he is angry until the next harvest."

A boy named Krodh asked her straight out. He was known for his temper. "When I get angry, it is like a flood. I am not in it. I am it. I say things. Afterwards I am sorry. How do you just stop?"

Nava thought about how to say it.

"When I play anger, I know three things. I know where it sits: here, between my brows, and here, in my jaw, and my breath goes short. I know its name. And I know that I am the one wearing it. It is like a coat. A coat can be heavy and hot. But you are not the coat."

"But yours is pretend. Mine is real."

"The feeling in my body is the same. The hot breath is real. Guru Amma says the only difference is that I see it coming. When you get angry, do your brows go down? Does your jaw go tight?"

Krodh thought. "My hands. They close up."

"Then next time, when your hands close up, say inside yourself, 'There it is. Anger has come. It is in my hands.' Just that. You do not have to push it away. Only see it and name it. A thing that you can see and name is a thing that you are standing a little outside of."

Krodh was not sure. But he tried it, and he found that between "there it is" and the shouting there was now a small gap. Sometimes, in that gap, he could choose.

On the sixth day there was a real quarrel in Rangpur.

Two families shared a wall, and the wall had fallen in the rains. Each said the other must mend it. It began with words. By afternoon the whole lane was shouting. Old things were dragged up. Somebody's grandfather had once borrowed a plough. A stick was picked up.

The players were resting nearby. Nava watched. She saw that nobody in the lane was listening. Each side could see only the other's angry face.

She walked into the middle of the lane. She was small, and in her stage paint. People paused.

She turned to the first family. And she became the mother of the second family. Not her words. Her feeling. Nava's shoulders dropped. Her hands twisted in her scarf. Her eyes went to the broken wall and then to an imagined child in the yard beyond it. Her face said, as clearly as words: I am afraid. The wall is down, the goats will get in, the baby will get out on the road, and I do not have the money to mend it, and I am ashamed to say so.

The first family went quiet. They had seen only anger on that woman. They had not seen what was under it.

Nava turned to the second family. Now she became the old father of the first. Her back bent. Her jaw set, proud. Her hand went to her chest, and her eyes were hurt. Her face said: Forty years I have been your neighbour. I carried your son to the healer on my back. And today you shouted at me in front of the whole lane, as if I were a thief.

Somebody in the second family made a small sound.

Then Nava did one more thing. She played both of them at the same moment as they had looked two minutes ago: red, puffed up, each with one finger pointing, like two angry roosters. She hopped from one spot to the other, pointing at herself.

It was so exact, and so silly, that a child laughed. Then the old father laughed, a short bark, against his will. Then the mother put her scarf over her mouth, and her shoulders shook.

In a quarter of an hour the two families were lifting stones together. The stick was used to mix mud.

That night, the seventh, Nava danced the ninth mood. Peace. It is the hardest one to play, Guru Amma said, because there is nothing to show. You only have to let everything else go.

Rangpur sat in the dark and watched her do nothing, very beautifully, for a long time.

## 5.9 The Rope Fence

The village of Bagh planted young trees every year: neem, mango and jamun, raised from seed. And every year the goats ate them.

"We need a nursery," said the elders. "A fenced place where seedlings can grow for two years, until they are too tall for goats."

Thorn branches for the fence were plentiful. But to plan it, the weaver gave them one long rope. "The fence will be as long as this rope. Forty steps. Thorn fence takes a week of work for every ten steps, and four weeks is all the hands we can spare. So forty steps of fence, and no more."

They gave the rope to Gheri, who was ten, and looked after the seedlings. "Mark out the nursery. You know how much room a seedling needs."

Gheri knew. Each seedling needed a square of ground, one step each way. So she wanted the fence to hold as many square steps as it could.

The first place that came to mind was the strip along the path. It was easy to reach. She laid the rope out in a long, thin shape: eighteen steps long and two steps wide. Eighteen and two and eighteen and two make forty. The rope was used up.

She counted the ground inside. Two rows of eighteen. Thirty-six seedlings.

"That is few," she thought. "A long thin shape is nearly all fence and no inside."

She tried a triangle, with three equal sides. Each side was a bit more than thirteen steps. She walked it and counted squares, as well as she could with the slanting edges. About seventy-seven.

"Better! Same rope, twice the room. So the shape matters. A great deal."

She tried a square. Ten steps each side. Ten rows of ten. One hundred.

"Better still. The triangle has sharp corners. The square has wider corners. The strip had its sides squeezed together. The more I open the shape out, the more it holds."

She sat down and thought about corners. A triangle has three, and they are sharp. A square has four, less sharp. What about a shape with six sides? She laid it out. The corners were wider still. She counted. About one hundred and fifteen.

"More sides, wider corners, more room. So what if it has no corners at all?"

She drove a peg into the ground. She did not know how long to make the string from the peg, so she tried. With a little more than six steps of string, a walk round the peg used up just forty steps of rope. She laid the rope in the circle and counted squares, and parts of squares at the edge, very carefully.

About one hundred and twenty-seven.

"The circle holds the most," she said. "The same rope. Thirty-six in the strip. One hundred and twenty-seven in the circle. If I had not tried, we would have built the strip, and lost ninety trees."

She was about to go and tell the elders. But something made her stop. Her grandmother had a saying: "When you have found the answer, ask once more."

"The circle is the best shape for a rope by itself," thought Gheri. "But is the rope by itself? What else is here?"

She looked round. On the east side of the ground ran the river. Its bank was high and steep, twice the height of a goat, and straight for a long way. No goat had ever climbed it.

"The bank is already a fence. A fence that I do not have to build! If I use the bank as one side, all forty steps of my rope can go to the other sides."

She tried a square against the bank first. The bank was one side, so the rope made only three. She found the best was ten steps out, twenty along and ten back. Two hundred squares. Twice the plain square!

Then she remembered about corners. She fixed one end of the rope at the bank. She walked out in a curve and came back to the bank farther along. Half a circle, with the river bank as its straight side.

She counted twice, because she did not believe it.

About two hundred and fifty-four.

"Twice the full circle. With the same rope. Because the river does half the work."

That evening she showed the elders: first the strip, then the square, then the circle, then the half circle on the bank. She walked each one and said the count.

"Thirty-six. One hundred. One hundred and twenty-seven. Two hundred and fifty-four."

The elders looked at each other. "We have only one hundred and fifty seedlings this year," said one.

"Then there is room for a hundred more next year," said Gheri. "Or..."

"Or?"

"There is no fenced place in the village where the little children can play without goats and carts. The seedlings can have the side by the water. The children can have the side by the gate. It is the same fence."

That is what they did. The trees from that nursery are tall now. The children who played beside them as seedlings bring their own children to sit in the shade.

At the gate there is a peg with a faded rope tied to it, forty steps long. Under it somebody has scratched Gheri's grandmother's words: "When you have found the answer, ask once more."

## 5.10 The Monster That Ate the Moon

It happened on a clear night in the month after harvest. The Moon was full and high. Then a dark bite appeared on its edge.

The bite grew. Slowly, the whole bright Moon was swallowed, and what was left hung in the sky dim and red, like a coal.

The town of Chandpur went wild with fear. People ran out with pots and pans and beat them. Dogs howled. Children cried. "A monster is eating the Moon! Make noise! Frighten it off!"

A man in a yellow coat climbed on the well. "I know this monster!" he cried. "I know the words that make it spit the Moon out. But the words are costly to say. One coin from every house, quickly, or the Moon is gone for ever!"

Coins rained into his bag. He waved his arms and shouted strange words. An hour later the Moon came back, as bright as before. People wept and thanked him. He left in the morning with a heavy bag.

One girl had not beaten a pot. Her name was Jyotsna, and she had sat on her roof through the whole thing, watching, with a lamp and a bundle of palm leaves.

The palm leaves were her family's sky diary. Her great-grandmother had begun it. Every clear night, somebody in the family had written down what the sky did: where the Moon rose, which stars stood near it and anything strange. There were sixty years of leaves.

Jyotsna had written three things tonight.

First: the bite did not come suddenly. It crept across the Moon at a steady pace, and it crept back off at the same pace. "A monster would chew," she wrote. "This is smooth, like a lid sliding."

Second: the edge of the dark part was curved. Always curved, from start to end, like the edge of a plate.

Third: it went away by itself. She had timed it by her lamp. It was leaving before the man in the yellow coat said a single word.

Next day she went through the old leaves. It took her many days. She was looking for the words "the Moon went dark".

She found them eleven times in sixty years. Then she laid those eleven leaves in a row and looked at what else each one said.

Every time, the Moon had been full. Never a half Moon, never a thin Moon. Only on a full-Moon night.

"When is the Moon full?" she asked herself. She knew that from watching. "When the Sun sets in the west just as the Moon rises in the east. The Sun on one side, the Moon on the other, and we in the middle."

She sat up straight.

"We in the middle. The Sun's light goes past us to reach the Moon. So what if we are in the way? Everything in the Sun has a shadow. The tree has one. I have one. The Earth must have one too, a huge one, pointing away from the Sun. And on some full-Moon nights the Moon walks straight into it."

That explained the smooth sliding. And the curved edge. It was the edge of the Earth's own shadow.

But why not every full Moon? She did not know. She guessed that the Moon's path was a little tilted, so that most months it passed above or below the shadow. It was only a guess, and she wrote it down as a guess.

Then she saw something in the dates that made her heart thump.

One darkening was written in her great-grandmother's hand. Another was written by her grandmother, eighteen years and eleven days later. Another was written by her mother, eighteen years and eleven days after that. And the same gap showed between other pairs.

"It repeats. Eighteen years and eleven days. If that is a true pattern, then I can say when the next one comes." She counted forward from a darkening that her mother had written down. The answer fell on a full-Moon night in the coming spring.

She told her family. She told her teacher. Her teacher said, "Say it in the square. If you are wrong, you will learn something. If you are right, the town will."

So Jyotsna stood in the square, with her knees shaking, and said: "On the full-Moon night of the second month of spring, the Moon will go dark again. I do not know at what hour. It is not a monster. It is our own Earth's shadow. It will pass by itself. You do not need to pay anyone. I am telling you now, months before, so that you can see whether I am right."

Some people laughed. Some were angry. "Do not call the monster's name!"

And three days before that night, the man in the yellow coat came back. He had a diary of his own, it seems.

"I feel the monster stirring!" he cried. "Have your coins ready!"

"She has already told us the night," said the baker. "For nothing. Tell us, sir. Do your words bring the Moon back, or does it come back by itself?"

"My words! Only my words!"

"Then stay silent this time," said Jyotsna. "If the Moon stays dark, everyone will pay you double. I will pay first."

He looked at the faces all around him. Then he looked at the road out of town.

On the night, the people of Chandpur carried mats to their roofs. Nobody carried a pot. The dark edge slid on to the Moon, smooth and curved.

"Look at the edge," said Jyotsna to the children near her. "It is always round. Whichever way the shadow falls, it is round. Only one shape makes a round shadow from every side."

"A ball," said a small boy.

"So that is the shape of what we are standing on," said Jyotsna.

The Moon went red as a coal. Nobody cried. An hour later it came out, bright and whole, and the town of Chandpur clapped. Not to frighten anything. Just because it was beautiful, and because they had known that it would come.

## 5.11 The Empty Place

This happened in the days when people had learned to write numbers with nine signs, one to nine, and to give each sign a value by its place. A three in the last place meant three. A three one place to the left meant thirty. A three in the next place meant three hundred. It was a wonderful way of writing, and it had one gap in it. A real gap.

In the caravan town of Registan, a clerk named Munshi kept the records for the biggest caravan of the year. He was quick and neat, and he was rude to the stable boy.

The stable boy had no parents. Nobody remembered his real name. He slept in the straw and fed the camels. Munshi called him Kuch-Nahin, which means "Nothing".

"Out of my way, Nothing." "Who asked you, Nothing?" The boy had heard it so often that he answered to it.

The night before the great caravan left, Munshi wrote the order for camel feed. The caravan had three hundred and two camels. Three hundreds. No tens. Two ones.

How do you write "no tens"? There was no sign for it. The custom was to leave the tens place empty. So Munshi wrote a 3, left a small space, and wrote a 2.

He was in a hurry. The space was not very wide.

The order went to the grain store. The grain keeper was reading by a poor lamp. He saw a 3 and a 2. "Thirty-two camels. A small caravan this year." He set out thirty-two camels' worth of feed for a forty-day crossing of the desert.

At dawn the feed sacks stood stacked by the gate. Three hundred and two camels were being loaded with silk and copper. Merchants shouted. Bells rang. In an hour the caravan would be out in the sand, where there is no grain to be had for forty days.

Kuch-Nahin was the one who carried the sacks to the feed camels. He could not read. But he could count. He had counted camels every day of his life.

He looked at the heap of sacks. He looked at the long, long line of camels, stretching out of the gate.

"That is not enough," he said.

Nobody heard him. He ran to Munshi.

"Sir. The feed. There is too little."

"Out of my way, Nothing. The order is written. Can you read?"

"No, sir. But I can count. One camel eats one sack in four days. That heap is three hundred and twenty sacks. I counted it twice. That is right for thirty-two camels. There are three hundred and two camels at the gate. I fed them last night. On that heap, they will eat for four days. Then there are thirty-six days of sand."

Munshi stopped walking. He was rude, but he was not stupid. He went white.

He snatched the order from the grain keeper. "I wrote three hundred and two!"

"You wrote thirty-two," said the grain keeper. "Look. Three. Two."

"There is a space between them! The space is the tens!"

"I see no space. I see a three and a two, written by a man in a hurry."

They both looked at it. It could be read either way. That was the terrible thing. A space is nothing, and nothing is very easy to miss.

The caravan master was called. The departure was stopped. It took the whole day to bring up the rest of the feed. If the caravan had left on time, three hundred camels and fifty people would have been deep in the desert when the sacks ran out.

That evening Munshi sat with his head in his hands. The boy was sweeping nearby.

"All my life I have left the empty place empty," said Munshi. "It nearly killed fifty people. But what else can one do? You cannot write nothing."

The boy leaned on his broom. He had been thinking about it all day.

"Why not, sir? When a stall in the stable is empty, I do not leave a gap in the row. If I did, the camels would shuffle up and I would lose count. I tie a rag on the post. The rag means: this stall is here, and no camel is in it. The rag is not a camel. It only keeps the place."

Munshi lifted his head.

"Put a mark in the empty place, sir. Any small mark. A dot. It is worth no camels at all. But it holds the place open, so the three cannot slide over and sit next to the two."

Munshi took his pen. He wrote a 3. He made a small round dot. He wrote a 2.

3 · 2.

Nobody in the world could read that as thirty-two.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he looked at the boy he had been calling Nothing.

"A mark that is worth nothing by itself," he said slowly. "But in the right place, it makes a three into three hundred. Take it out, and two hundred and seventy camels vanish." He put down the pen. "I owe you more than one kind of apology. What is your name?"

"I do not know, sir. I only know the one you gave me."

"Then, if you will allow it, we shall keep it. But we shall say it differently. Among the scholars there is an old word for the empty: shunya. I have called you 'nothing' as an insult. From today it is a title. You are the one who holds the place."

The boy called Shunya learned to read that year. Munshi taught him, and was never rude to him again. The little dot spread from Registan along every caravan road. In time people stopped thinking of it as a mark for a gap and began to wonder whether it might be a number in its own right.

But that is another story, and it comes later.

## 5.12 The Boy Who Could Not Sit Still

Chanchal was nine, and his body did not have a still place in it. His foot tapped. His fingers drummed. His eyes jumped from the bird to the cart to the cloud. When someone spoke to him, he heard the first half and then a goat walked by.

"That boy has a monkey inside him," said the people of the caravan. They said it fondly. But Chanchal knew that it was true, and it troubled him. He lost things. He forgot what he had been told. At night his thoughts ran round and round, and he could not sleep.

The caravan was crossing the dry country, thirty camels and twenty people. Among them was an old traveller named Thira. She walked slowly and missed nothing. She would say, "Rain tomorrow," or, "Somebody has dropped a waterskin, four camels back," and she was always right.

One evening Chanchal sat down beside her. For him, that meant sitting, getting up, sitting and digging a hole with his heel.

"How do you notice everything?" he asked.

"I am not doing anything else," said Thira.

"I notice too many things. All at once. And then none of them."

Thira looked at him. "Would you like a game? It is harder than it sounds. Most grown people cannot do it."

Chanchal liked hard games.

"Sit so that your back is straight but not stiff. Close your eyes, or look at the ground. Now. Your breath goes in and out all day and you have never once watched it. Watch it. When it goes out, count: one. Next time it goes out: two. Count ten breaths. That is all."

"That is easy."

"If your mind goes away and thinks about something else, and you lose the count, you must go back to one."

Chanchal shut his eyes. One. Two. This is easy. I wonder what is for dinner. Was that a camel?

"I lost it," he said, astonished.

"Where did you get to?"

"Two!"

Thira laughed, but kindly. "Most people do. Again."

He tried again. He got to three, and then he was thinking about a lizard that he had seen at noon. Again. Four, and then a song was in his head. He grew angry with himself.

"Do not be angry," said Thira. "This is the heart of the game. Your mind went off after the lizard. That is what minds do. But then something noticed that it had gone. Who noticed?"

Chanchal thought. "I did."

"So there is the mind that runs about. And there is something that can watch it run. Like you watching a bird. The bird hops here and there, and you sit and see it. Every time you notice that you have lost the count, that is the watcher waking up. You are not failing. You are practising the waking up. Go back to one, gently, the way you would lead a small goat back to the path. No hitting."

Chanchal played the game every evening. For a week he never got past five. Then one evening he got to ten. He opened his eyes, and the world looked oddly clear, as if somebody had wiped it.

He did not turn into a still boy. His foot still tapped. But something had changed. When his thoughts began to race at night, he could now see them racing. He could say, "There they go," and count his breath until they got tired.

He began to notice things, too. Not everything at once, but one thing, and then the next.

In the fourth week the dust storm came.

It came in the afternoon, a brown wall as high as the sky, with a sound like a river. The caravan master shouted, "Camels down! Everyone down, hold a rope!" But Chanchal and four other children had gone a little way off to look for firewood. The wall hit them before they could get back.

The world went away. There was only roaring brown, and sand in the mouth, and sand in the eyes. Chanchal could not see his own hand.

The other children screamed. One began to run. "This way! I think it was this way!"

"No, this way!" Another ran the opposite way.

Chanchal's heart was going like a drum. His legs wanted to run, anywhere, fast. His mind was shouting ten things at once.

And then the watcher woke up. "There it goes," it said. "Fear. In my legs and in my chest. Shouting."

He caught the nearest two children by their clothes. "Sit down! Do not run. If we all run different ways, we will lose each other and the caravan. Sit. Hold on to me. Call the others back."

They called. The two who had run could still hear, and they crawled back, crying. Five children sat in a knot with their scarves over their faces.

"Now be quiet," said Chanchal. "All of you. I need to listen."

He straightened his back. He shut his eyes, for they were no use anyway. And he counted his breath going out. One. Two.

At first there was only the roar. Three. Four. The fear was still there, but it sat beside him now, not on top of him. Five. Six. The roar was not one sound. It had layers. There was the high hiss of the sand. There was the low moan of the wind. Seven. Eight.

And under them, very faint, from somewhere to his left, came something that was not wind. A dull clonk. A pause. Clonk, clonk.

Camel bells. The camels were lying down. But a camel shakes its head when sand blows in its ears.

He waited through ten more breaths to be sure. The sound stayed in the same place. To the left, and a little behind.

"Hold hands," he said. "A chain. Nobody lets go. I am in front. We go slowly, and we stop every ten steps so that I can listen."

They went ten steps. He stopped, breathed and listened. Clonk. A little louder. Ten more steps. Stop. Listen. Louder still.

After a hundred and fifty steps, his knee bumped into something large, warm and hairy, which groaned at him.

Hands reached out of the brown and pulled all five children in under a blanket.

When the storm had passed, the caravan master counted heads twice and then sat down hard on the sand. "Who brought you in?"

The four pointed at Chanchal.

"The monkey boy?" said the caravan master. "He sat still?"

Thira was shaking the sand out of her shawl. She did not look surprised in the least.

"He has been practising," she said.
