## 2.1 The Seed That Was Told to Hurry

Gauri's mother gave her five seeds. "These are yours," she said. "Plant them, give them water, and wait."

Gauri made five small holes behind the house. She put one seed in each hole. But the fifth seed slipped from her fingers and rolled away down the path. She looked and looked. She could not find it.

"Never mind," said Gauri. "Four is enough." She covered the four seeds and gave them water.

The next morning she ran to look. Nothing. Only brown earth.

"Hurry up," she told the seeds.

She could not wait. She dug up the first seed with her finger. It looked just the same. She pushed it back.

The next morning, nothing. She dug up the second seed. It had a tiny white tail coming out of it! "Oh!" said Gauri. She turned it over and over in her hand to look. Then she pushed it back.

The next day she dug up the third seed. And the day after, the fourth. And then the first again. Every morning she dug and looked and said, "Hurry up!"

Many days went by. Nothing came up. The seed with the white tail had gone soft and brown.

Gauri sat down on the path, very sad. And there, right beside her foot, she saw something green.

A little plant, as tall as her finger, with two round leaves.

"Who planted you?" said Gauri. Then she remembered. The fifth seed! The one that rolled away. Nobody had dug it up. Nobody had told it to hurry. It had only had rain and Sun and time.

Gauri thought about that for a long while.

"My four seeds were trying to grow," she said. "Every day they began, and every day I pulled them out to look. I did not let them do their work."

She asked her mother for four more seeds. She planted them. She gave them water. And this time she put a ring of small stones round them and sat on her hands.

One day. Nothing. She did not dig.

Two days. Nothing. She did not dig. It was very hard.

On the sixth day, four green backs were pushing up through the earth, like four small children getting out of bed.

"Good morning," said Gauri softly. "Take your time."

## 2.2 The Pot That Could Not Go Back

Kamli's father was a potter. Every day he took wet clay and made pots, round and smooth. Then he baked them in a hot fire until they were hard.

"Can I make one?" asked Kamli.

"When your hands are bigger," said her father. "Until then, play with the clay."

Playing with clay was fun. Kamli made a ball. She did not like it, so she squashed it and made a snake. She squashed the snake and made a cup. With wet clay you can always squash it and start again.

One afternoon her father went to the river. The fire for baking was still hot. Kamli made a little pot, all by herself. It was not very round. One side of the top was pinched out, like a small beak, where her thumb had slipped.

"I will bake it," she thought. "Then I will have a real pot." She put it at the edge of the fire.

When the fire went cold, she took it out. It was hard. It was a real pot. But oh, that crooked beak! Next to her father's pots it looked so funny.

"I will squash it and make it again," said Kamli. She pressed. Nothing. She pressed harder. Nothing. She put it in water to make it soft. It stayed as hard as stone.

Her father came back and found her crying.

"It will not go back," she said. "I want to make it again and it will not go back to clay."

Her father sat beside her. "No. Fire changes clay for ever. Wet clay forgives. Baked clay does not. That is why I look three times before I bake."

"So what do I do?"

"What is done is done. Now we find out what it is good for."

That evening Mother was pouring water from a big round pot into the cups. As always, water ran down the side of the pot and splashed on the floor.

Kamli looked at her crooked little pot. She filled it and tipped it. The water ran along the little beak and fell in one thin, clean line, straight into the cup. Not one drop on the floor.

Mother stared. Father stared. He took the pot and poured. Then he poured again.

The next day Kamli's father made twenty pots. Before he baked them, he pinched the top of each one with his thumb, just so.

"What do you call that?" asked a neighbour.

"Kamli's beak," said the potter.

## 2.3 The Square Wheel

Long ago, in one village, nobody had ever seen a wheel. People pulled heavy loads on a flat sledge. It scraped along the ground, and it was hard work.

One day a boy named Bira found four flat square pieces of wood. He put a stick through the middle of each and fixed them under the sledge.

"Now it will roll," said Bira.

His friends pulled. The cart stood up on a corner, and then, THUMP, it fell flat on a side. Up on a corner. THUMP. Up. THUMP. The pots on the cart jumped. The baby on the cart cried.

"It does roll," said Bira. "But it thumps."

"The corners are the trouble," said his sister Somi. "Let us have fewer corners. Three!"

They cut pieces with three corners. That was worse. The cart climbed up, up, up on a point and came down with a BANG.

"Wrong way," said Somi. "Fewer corners, bigger thump. So let us try more corners."

They cut pieces with six corners. Thump-thump-thump. The thumps were smaller and quicker. The baby stopped crying.

"More corners, smaller thump!" said Bira.

They cut pieces with twelve corners. It took all day. Now the cart went tup-tup-tup-tup. The pots only shivered a little. The baby laughed.

All the children came to help. They cut off every corner they could see. Each time they cut one corner, two smaller corners were left. So they cut those too. And those too.

Tup-tup-tup became trrrrrr. And then it became nothing at all. The cart ran so smoothly that the baby fell asleep.

Everyone looked at the wheel. No sides. No points. Just one smooth edge going round and round.

Little Tinku, the smallest, put his finger on it.

"How many corners does it have now?" he asked.

"None," said Bira. "We cut them all off."

"No," said Somi slowly. "We never took corners away. Every time we cut, there were more corners, only smaller. So it has more corners than anybody can count. So many and so small that you cannot feel them."

"None," said Bira.

"More than anyone can count," said Somi.

Tinku rolled the wheel along the ground and thought about it. He is still thinking. What do you say?

## 2.4 The Flute That Cost a Song

Veni wanted a flute more than anything in the world.

She went to the flute maker. "Please, may I have a flute?"

"What will you give me for it?" asked the flute maker. Veni had nothing. "Bring me a bowl of honey," he said, "and the flute is yours."

Veni went to the honey man. "Please, may I have a bowl of honey?"

"What will you give me?" he asked. "My climbing rope is old and thin. Bring me a new rope, and the honey is yours."

Veni went to the rope woman. "Please, may I have a rope?"

"What will you give me?" she asked. "My water pot is cracked. Bring me a new pot, and the rope is yours."

Veni went to the potter. He was walking up and down with a crying baby on his shoulder.

"Please, may I have a pot?"

"What will you give me?" said the tired potter. "Ah, I know what I want. My baby has not slept for two days. Only music makes her sleep. Play her a tune on a flute, and the pot is yours."

Veni's mouth fell open. A tune on a flute! To get the flute she needed honey. For honey, a rope. For a rope, a pot. For a pot, a flute. It went round in a circle, like a dog running after its own tail.

She sat down and thought. A circle has no beginning. So somebody must begin.

She ran back to the flute maker. "I have no honey yet. But lend me a flute until evening. I give you my word, I will come back with the honey or with the flute."

The flute maker looked at her for a long time. "A word is a thing too," he said. "Take it."

Veni ran to the potter and played. It was not a very good tune. It was her first. But it was soft and slow, and the baby's eyes closed.

The potter gave her a pot. She carried it to the rope woman, who gave her a rope. She carried the rope to the honey man, who gave her a bowl of honey. And before the Sun went down, she put the honey in the flute maker's hands.

"You kept your word," he said. "So the flute is yours."

That night the potter's baby slept. The rope woman had water. The honey man climbed his tree. And all down the lane, they could hear Veni learning her second tune.

## 2.5 The Missing Bead

Grandmother had a necklace of red and blue beads. It went like this: red, red, blue. Red, red, blue. Red, red, blue. All the way round.

One day the string broke. Beads rolled everywhere, under the bed, behind the pots, out of the door.

"Do not worry, Grandmother," said Meena. "We will find them all." She and her little brother Golu crawled about and picked up every bead they could see.

Meena began to thread them on a new string, the same way as before. Red, red, blue. Red, red, blue. Red, red, blue. Then she picked up one red bead. And the bowl was empty.

"Red..." said Meena. "And then nothing. After red comes one more red. And after that, a blue. The necklace is telling me that two beads are lost. One red and one blue."

"How can it tell you?" asked Golu.

"Because it goes the same way every time. If you know how it goes, you know what is missing."

Golu went quiet. He looked at the floor. Then he slowly opened his hand. In it was a red bead, warm and a little wet.

"I kept it," he said in a small voice. "It was so pretty. I am sorry."

"Thank you for telling," said Meena, and she put it on the string. "Red, red. Now only the blue one."

They looked under the bed again. They looked behind the pots. No blue bead.

"It rolled out of the door," said Golu. "I saw something shiny go out."

Outside, on the neem tree, sat a magpie. She tipped her head and looked at them with one bright eye. In the fork of the tree was her big untidy nest.

"Did the magpie take it?" said Meena. "Let us look before we say." She climbed up carefully, branch by branch, and looked in.

"Golu! Come and see!"

In the nest lay the blue bead. But not only the blue bead. There was Father's lost fish hook. There was Auntie's little copper ring. There was a shell button, a bit of bright stone and the neighbour's needle, the one the whole lane had hunted for.

They took the things that belonged to people and left the magpie her bit of bright stone.

That evening Grandmother wore her necklace. Red, red, blue, all the way round. And Meena and Golu went from door to door down the lane, giving back lost things.

## 2.6 Ten Fingers, Eleven Sheep

Dhanu looked after his family's sheep. Every evening he counted them on his fingers. One sheep, one finger.

For a long time there were ten sheep. Ten sheep, ten fingers. Easy.

Then one spring a lamb was born. That evening Dhanu counted. One finger, two fingers, all the way to the last finger. And one sheep was still standing there, looking at him.

"I have no more fingers!" said Dhanu.

He thought. Then he picked up a stone and put it on the ground. "This stone means two full hands," he said. He opened his hands again. "Now my fingers are free." He put up one finger for the lamb.

One stone and one finger. That was his flock.

Years went by and the flock grew. More lambs, more stones. Now when Dhanu counted, he had four stones and three fingers.

One day a stranger came over the hill with his own sheep. His name was Bodo, and he came from far away. They let the two flocks drink at the same pool, and they sat down together.

"How many sheep have you?" asked Bodo.

"Four stones and three fingers," said Dhanu proudly.

Bodo walked among Dhanu's sheep and counted in his own way. It was a funny way. He touched his fingers, and then he bent down and touched his toes! When he had touched all his fingers and all his toes, he put down a stick.

"You have two sticks and three fingers," said Bodo.

"No. Four stones and three fingers."

"Two sticks."

"Four stones!"

They looked at each other. Somebody had to be wrong. But who?

"Let us see," said Dhanu. They made the sheep walk through a narrow gap, one at a time. As each sheep went through, Dhanu put up a finger and Bodo touched a finger or a toe.

When Dhanu's hands were full, he put down a stone. Bodo kept going on his toes. When Dhanu's hands were full again, he put down a second stone. And at that very moment Bodo put down his first stick.

"Oh!" they said together.

"Your stick is two of my stones," said Dhanu. "You count hands and feet. I count only hands."

"Same sheep," said Bodo. "Different way."

When the last sheep went through, there lay four stones on one side and two sticks on the other. And both of them had three fingers up.

Nobody was wrong. They laughed, and each one taught the other his way.

## 2.7 The Frog Who Was Wrong Once

In Minu's village, everybody knew the rule. When the frogs sing, the rain is coming, and it is time to put the seed in the ground.

One hot evening the frogs sang. Trr-trr-trr, all night long.

"Tomorrow we sow," said the elders.

But Minu had been watching something else all summer. There was an ants' nest under the mango tree. Minu had seen that before every rain, the ants carried their small white eggs up the tree, away from the ground. Today the ants were not carrying anything.

Minu was small, and the elders were big. Her heart beat fast. But she went and stood before them.

"Please. The frogs are singing, but the ants have not moved their eggs. I do not think the rain is coming yet."

Some people laughed. But old Kaki did not. "The child has been watching," she said. "Let us be careful. We will sow half the seed tomorrow and keep half."

They sowed half. No rain came. For ten days the Sun burned, and the seed in the ground dried up. The frogs had been wrong. But half the seed was safe in the pots.

Now everybody looked at Minu in a new way. "Tell us when the ants move," they said.

A few days later Minu came running. "They are carrying the eggs! All of them! The rain is coming!"

The village got ready. They waited. No rain came that day, and none the next.

Minu's face was hot. She ran back to the tree. And there she saw why. A goat had stepped right on the nest and broken it. The ants were not running from rain. They were running from a goat.

She told the elders the truth. "The ants were wrong this time. No. I was wrong. I saw them move and did not ask why."

Old Kaki nodded. "The frog can be wrong. The ant can be wrong. Each one is only a sign. A sign is not the rain."

"Then what do we do?" asked Minu.

"What do you think?"

Minu thought. "One sign can be wrong. But it is not easy for two signs to be wrong on the same day. Let us wait until the frogs sing and the ants climb."

Eight days later the frogs sang all night. In the morning the ants were going up the mango tree in a long white line.

"Now," said Minu.

They sowed the seed. That night the rain came down, and it rained for three days.

## 2.8 The Mouse Who Guarded the Seed

In a corner of the village grain store lived a family of mice. All winter there was grain, and the mice were fat and happy.

But spring came, and the heap of grain grew small. At last there was only one small pile left.

"Let us eat it!" said Motu. Motu was the greediest mouse of all. "I am hungry now. Tomorrow we will find something."

"Wait," said his cousin Chunni. She was a careful mouse. "I have watched the people. When they have a little grain left, they do not eat it. They put it in the ground. And later they come back with baskets and baskets."

"Put food in the mud? That is silly," said Motu, and he began to eat.

Chunni could not stop him. But she took twenty grains in her cheeks, ten on this side and ten on that side. She ran out to the soft earth by the wall and pushed each grain into the ground. Then she sat and guarded them.

It was hard. Her tummy made noises. The grains were right there, under her feet. Every day Motu came by. "Just one," he said. "Let us eat just one."

"No," said Chunni. "Not one."

Rain fell. Green shoots came up. They grew tall, and at the top of each one came a fat ear, heavy with grain.

Chunni climbed up one and counted the grains on it. She used all her toes and had to start again, and again, and again.

"Motu," she said, "I planted one grain here. Now there are more than I can count. And there are twenty plants."

Motu looked up at the plants. He looked for a long time. He was thinking of the pile he had eaten in one night.

"If I had kept my grains," he said slowly, "I would have this many too."

"Yes," said Chunni.

That year every mouse had enough, and Motu most of all, for Chunni shared with everyone. Then the family had to choose who would guard the seed for next spring.

"Motu," said Chunni.

"Me?" said Motu. "But I am the one who wants to eat it!"

"That is why. You know just how it feels to want it. Nobody will trick you with 'just one'. You have heard it before. You said it yourself."

So Motu sat on the seed all winter. Many times a small mouse came and said, "Just one?"

And Motu said, "Not one. Come back in summer. Then I will give you a hundred."

## 2.9 The Drum Message

Three villages stood along a river. One was high in the hills. One was in the middle. One was down by the wide water.

They talked to each other with drums. The hill village beat its drum, the middle village heard it and beat the same on its own drum, and so the message went down to the river village.

Every beat had a meaning. Two beats, dum-dum, meant "Bring the goats." Three beats, dum-dum-dum, meant "Bring the boats."

One day the hill village was getting ready for a fair. "Tell the river village to bring their goats to the fair," said the headman. The drummer beat the big drum.

Dum-dum.

In the middle village, a boy named Jeetu was the drummer that day. He heard it. Dum-dum. But just then, from the cliff behind him, came the echo of the last beat. Dum.

"Dum-dum... dum," counted Jeetu. "Three." He beat three on his drum.

Dum-dum-dum.

Down by the river, the people looked at each other. "Bring the boats? Up to the hills? Why?"

"The drum said boats," said an old woman. "They must have a reason. We bring boats."

So they pulled their boats up along the river path, all day, puffing and wondering. By evening the boats lay in a row beside the hill village.

"Boats?" said the headman. "We asked for goats!" Everybody laughed, the river people most of all.

But that night it rained in the mountains. The river rose in the dark. By morning brown water was running through the low fields, and three families were sitting on their roofs.

And there were the boats, right there, ready. Everybody was brought to dry ground before the Sun was high.

"Well," said the headman. "A wrong message did a good thing. But that was only luck. Next time a wrong message could do harm. How do we stop it?"

Jeetu stood up. His ears were red. "It was me. I counted the echo as a beat. I am sorry."

"Anyone can hear wrong," said the old woman from the river. "The trouble is that nobody checked."

Jeetu thought hard. "What if the one who hears the message beats it back first? If I hear two, I beat two back up to the hills. If the hills say yes, then I send it on. If I heard wrong, they will know at once."

From that day the drums spoke twice. Once to tell, and once to say, "Is this what you said?"

And the goats came to the fair one week late.

## 2.10 The Pup Who Chose

At the edge of the village, by the thorn bushes, the children found a wild pup. He was thin and grey, with big ears, and he shook when they came near.

"He is mine," said Raka. "I saw him first."

"I want to be his friend too," said Juhi.

Raka ran home and came back with a rope. He caught the pup and tied the rope round his neck. He tied the other end to a post by his door. "Now he cannot go," said Raka. "Now he is my dog."

The pup pulled at the rope. He cried all night. When Raka came close to pat him, he showed his little teeth.

"Why does he not like me?" said Raka. "I gave him a place to stay."

Juhi watched and said nothing. She thought, "He does not know us. Why should he trust us? If a giant tied me to a post, I would not like the giant."

In the morning the rope lay on the ground, bitten through. The pup was gone, back to the thorn bushes.

That evening Juhi took a little milk and bread in a broken bowl. She put it down near the bushes. Then she walked away, a long way, and sat down with her back turned.

After a long time the pup crept out. He ate very fast and ran back.

The next evening she did the same. But she sat a little closer.

The next evening, a little closer. Every day the same time. Every day the same bowl. She never ran at him. She never put out her hand. On the sixth day the pup ate with one eye on her. On the ninth day he ate and then sat down and looked at her.

Raka came and sat beside her. "You are so slow," he whispered. "With a rope you have him in one moment."

"With a rope you have the rope," said Juhi. "I want him to come because he wants to."

"What if he never comes?"

"Then he never comes. It is for him to choose."

On the twelfth evening the pup finished his milk. He walked over, slowly, slowly. He sniffed Juhi's foot. He sniffed Raka's foot too. Raka held his breath and did not move.

That night Juhi left her door open, just a little.

In the morning, a small grey pup lay across the doorway, fast asleep, with his nose on his paws.

Nobody had tied him. He could go any time he liked. He stayed for fourteen years.

## 2.11 The Field With No Owner

Beside the village lay a big green field. It belonged to nobody, so it belonged to everybody. Ten families lived in the village, and each family sent two goats to eat grass there.

There was plenty of grass. The goats ate, and the grass grew back.

One day Hari's father thought, "If I send one more goat, I will have more milk. One more goat is nothing in such a big field."

He was right. One goat was nothing.

But Lata's mother saw it and thought the very same thing. So did the next family. And the next. "Just one more," said each one. "What can one goat do?"

Soon there were thirty goats in the field. Then forty.

The grass got shorter. Then there were brown spots. Then the brown spots joined together. One morning the children took the goats out, and there was no field. Only dust, and forty thin, hungry goats.

The grown-ups stood at the edge and shouted. "It was you! You sent the first one!" "I sent only one! You sent two!"

The children sat on the wall and looked at the dust.

"Nobody did a big wrong thing," said Lata. "Everybody did a small thing. But we all did the same small thing."

"The field does not count families," said Hari. "It counts mouths."

"Then we need a rule that everybody keeps," said Lata. "Not my rule or your rule. One rule for the field."

They drew it in the dust with a stick. They cut the field into four parts with lines of stones. "This moon, all the goats eat only in the first part. The other three parts rest. Next moon, the second part. And each family sends two goats again, as before."

The grown-ups stopped shouting and looked. It was hard to say yes, for two goats give less milk than four. But there was no milk at all in dust. One by one, they agreed.

The rains came. In the resting parts, green came back, thin at first and then thick.

And in the third part, where the ground had been hard and bare for so long, the children found a wet place. They moved a flat stone. Under it, clear cold water was coming up out of the earth. The goats' feet had kept it shut all these years.

Now the field had grass and a spring. It still belonged to nobody. So everybody looked after it.

## 2.12 Who Is Eating the Moon?

One night Chanda looked up, and the Moon was round and white, like a full bowl of milk.

The next night she looked again. A little bit was gone from one side.

The next night, a little more.

"Somebody is eating the Moon!" she told her grandfather. "Every night, one bite."

"Is that so?" said Grandfather. "Who?"

"I do not know. But I will catch him."

Chanda took a long string. Every night she went outside, looked at the Moon and tied one knot. One night, one knot.

The Moon got thinner. Half a Moon. Then a slice, like a piece of melon. Then a thin curved line, like a cut fingernail. Knot, knot, knot.

Then one night there was no Moon at all. The sky was black.

Chanda cried. "He has eaten all of it. I did not catch him." But she tied a knot, because she had promised herself. One night, one knot.

Two nights later, low in the evening sky, she saw it. A thin, thin line of light, curved the other way.

"It is coming back!"

Every night it was a little fatter. Knot, knot, knot. A slice. A half. And one night it was round and white again, a full bowl of milk.

Chanda looked at her string. It was full of knots, more than she could count.

"Nobody ate it," she said. "It goes and it comes back, by itself. Will it go again?"

"What do you think?" said Grandfather.

She took a new string and began again. And this time, each night, she laid the new string beside the old one, knot beside knot.

The Moon grew thin. It was thinnest just where the old string said it would be. It went dark at the very same knot. It came back at the very same knot.

"Grandfather! It does the same thing every time. The same number of nights!"

Now it was nearly harvest. The village wanted to hold its feast on the night of the full Moon, so that there would be light for dancing. "But which night will that be?" the people asked. "Last year we guessed wrong, and we danced in the dark."

Chanda put her two strings side by side. She put her finger on tonight's knot. She counted the knots that were left on the old string.

"Five more nights," she said.

They made ready. On the fifth night the Moon came up over the hill, round and white.

After that, the village kept Chanda's string on a peg by the grain store. And every evening, somebody tied a knot.
