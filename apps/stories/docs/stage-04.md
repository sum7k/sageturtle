## 4.1 The Rope With Twelve Knots

Sutra was eleven years old, and he carried bricks for the master builder of the town.

One day the master gave him a rope. It had twelve knots, all the same distance apart, and its two ends were tied together to make a ring.

"This is worth more than my hammer," said the master. "Hold it at three knots, so that one side has three spaces, one side has four and one side has five. Pull it tight."

Sutra and two other boys pulled. The rope made a triangle. And the corner between the short side and the middle side was a perfect square corner, as true as the corner of a brick.

"Three, four and five," said the master. "It gives a true corner every time. Not most times. Every time. Build your walls on it and they will stand."

Sutra could not believe that anything worked every time. So he tested it. He made the triangle in the yard, on the road, on the roof. He made it in the morning and in the evening. Every time, the same true corner.

Then he began to test walls. He laid the rope in the corner of his own house. A little crooked. He laid it in the corner of the potter's shop. Very crooked. That shop had a crack down one wall.

One evening, when nobody was there, he laid the rope at the corner of the town hall. Everyone was proud of that hall. The master himself had built it as a young man.

The corner was not true. It was off by the width of a thumb.

Sutra felt cold. He checked again. He turned the rope round and checked a third time. The rope did not change its answer.

Now what? He was a brick boy. The master was the master. Nobody would ever know if he just kept quiet. But one day people would build a second floor on that hall.

He did not sleep much. In the morning he went to the master.

"Sir, I laid the rope on the town hall. I think the east corner is not true. I may be wrong. Please come and see."

The master's face went dark. He picked up the rope and walked to the hall without a word. Some builders followed, whispering. Sutra walked behind, and his legs shook.

The master laid the rope himself. He looked for a long time.

Then he turned to the others. "The boy is right. I built this corner in a hurry forty years ago, on the last day before the rains, and I never checked it. He checked it." He put his hand on Sutra's shoulder. "The rope does not care who is the master and who carries bricks. Neither should we. Thank you."

That month they mended the east corner. Sutra carried the bricks. And the master gave him a twelve-knot rope of his own.

## 4.2 The Tree Too Tall to Climb

The oldest tree in the village was a great silk-cotton tree. Every year, on the night of the harvest festival, a lamp was hung from its top branch, so that people far away could see it and come.

The lamp went up on a rope. A thin string already hung over the top branch, left there from last year. You tied the new rope to the string and pulled it over. But this year the old rope had rotted, and the weaver had to make a new one.

"How long must it be?" asked the weaver. "Rope is costly. I will not make it twice."

Nobody knew. The rope had to go from the ground up to the top branch and down again. So it must be twice as long as the tree was tall. But how tall was the tree?

"I will climb it with a measuring cord," said a big boy named Veeru.

"You will not," said his mother. "That tree is as high as twenty men, and the bark is full of thorns."

A girl named Maapi had been listening. "We do not need to climb it," she said. "We can measure its shadow."

"A shadow is not a tree," said Veeru.

"No. But think. In the morning my shadow is longer than I am. At noon it is shorter. So at some moment in between, my shadow is just as long as I am. At that same moment, the Sun is making every shadow just as long as its owner. The tree's too. Then we walk along the tree's shadow and count."

Everyone thought this was clever. The next morning Maapi stood in the open, with a friend to mark her shadow.

But clouds came. Thick grey clouds, from one side of the sky to the other. No Sun. No shadows at all. And the festival was the next night.

Veeru looked at the tree again. "I can climb it," he said quietly.

"Wait," said Maapi. "Let me think once more."

She was looking down at a puddle from last night's rain. In the puddle, upside down, she could see the top of the tree.

She took a flat bowl of water and put it on the ground. She stepped back until she could see the tree's top branch right in the middle of the water. Then she moved the bowl, and stepped again, and moved it again, until she found the place she wanted.

"Here. Look. I am standing just as far from the bowl as my eyes are high. Measure it with a stick, you will see. The water is flat, so it shows the tree to me on the same slant as it shows me to the tree. So the tree's top is as high as the tree is far from the bowl."

They measured from the bowl to the foot of the tree. Forty-one steps. So the rope had to be eighty-two steps long, and a little more for the knots.

The weaver made it once. The next night the lamp went up, and the rope reached with two hands of length to spare.

Veeru held the end of it. "I am glad I did not have to climb," he told Maapi.

## 4.3 The Odd One at the Dance

In the Land of Pairs, everything went two by two.

People walked two by two. Houses had two doors. Shoes, of course, came in pairs, but so did hats. And at the spring dance, everybody danced in twos.

Every child in the village had a dance partner. Every child but one. There were nineteen children, and nineteen cannot be split into pairs. Nine pairs danced. One girl stood by the wall. Her name was Ekta.

"She is the odd one," people said. They did not say it in an unkind way. In the Land of Pairs, "odd" was just the word for one left over. But Ekta heard it every spring, and it did not feel like just a word.

She tried to mend it. One year she asked if three could dance together. "Three is not a pair," they said. One year she danced with a broom. Everybody laughed, and so did she, but not inside.

Then Ekta did what she always did when she was sad. She played with pebbles.

She laid out nineteen pebbles in twos. Nine pairs and one left. She laid out seven. Three pairs and one left. Eleven. Five pairs and one left.

"Some numbers always leave one," she said. "Seven, eleven, nineteen. They are like me."

Then she pushed two heaps together, by chance: the seven and the eleven. She made pairs. And there was nothing left over.

She sat very still. She tried five and nine. Nothing left over. Three and seven. Nothing left over.

"The one left over from this heap takes the hand of the one left over from that heap," she said. "Two odds together are always even!"

Across the river was another village of the Land of Pairs. Ekta had never been there. She walked there the next morning. And she asked a strange question: "How many children are in your dance?"

"Thirteen," they said. "Six pairs. And poor Akela, who stands by the wall."

Akela was a thin boy with a wide smile. When Ekta told him about the pebbles, his smile got wider.

That spring the two villages danced together. Nineteen and thirteen make thirty-two. Sixteen pairs. Nobody stood by the wall.

But Ekta and Akela were not done. "What happens next year," said Akela, "if a new baby is born in your village? Then we are odd again."

"Then we need a dance," said Ekta, "where the number does not matter at all."

They thought for a week. Then they showed everyone. All the children held hands in one big ring. The ring went round to the left, round to the right, in to the middle and out again.

In a circle, nobody needs a pair. Nineteen can dance it. Thirty-two can dance it. Even one more can always step in.

The Land of Pairs still dances two by two. But the last dance of the night is always the ring.

## 4.4 Seven Laddoos

Bhola's grandmother made laddoos. When she had finished, she gave Bhola a plate. "These are yours. Share them fairly with your friends."

Bhola counted. Seven laddoos.

His friend Chintu was waiting outside. "Two of us," said Bhola. "Three for you, three for me. And one is left."

"I will take the extra one," said Chintu.

"That is not fair," said Bhola.

"Then you take it."

"That is not fair either." Bhola really did want to be fair. "Let us call Rani. Then we are three."

Rani came. Bhola began to give them out. Two, two, two. And one was left.

They called Rani's brother. Now they were four. One each. Then there were three left for four children.

They called a fifth friend. One each, and two were left over.

They called a sixth. One each, and one was left over. That one laddoo sat alone on the plate, and six children looked at it.

"This is a very stubborn number," said Rani.

"We can break the last one into six bits," said Chintu.

"Have you ever tried to break a laddoo into six?" said Rani. "You get crumbs and a fight."

Bhola was thinking. "With six laddoos this would be easy. Six goes into two threes. Or three twos. Or six ones."

"And eight?" said Rani. "Two fours. Four twos."

"But seven does not break into anything. Not twos, not threes, not fours, not fives, not sixes. We tried them all."

"Maybe your grandmother counted wrong," said Chintu. "Maybe she meant to make six. Let me help by eating one."

"No," said Bhola. He was looking down the lane. A small girl was sitting on a doorstep. She was new in the village, and nobody had talked to her yet.

Bhola counted heads. One, two, three, four, five, six. He looked at the laddoos. Seven. Then he looked at the girl again and he began to smile.

"Seven does break into something," he said. "It breaks into seven ones. We do not have too many laddoos. We have too few friends!"

He ran down the lane. "Do you like laddoos? Come!"

Her name was Saatvi. She came, a little shy.

Seven children, seven laddoos. One each. Nothing left over, nothing broken and nobody cross.

That evening Bhola asked his grandmother, "Why did you make seven? Six is much easier to share."

Grandmother laughed. "Is that so? What did seven make you do?"

Bhola thought about it. "It made me find one more friend."

"There are other numbers like seven," said Grandmother. "Eleven. Thirteen. They will not be split into equal heaps. People call them prime. I call them party numbers. With them, the only fair way is one for everyone, so you must go and find everyone."

Next week she made eleven.

## 4.5 The Line Nobody Could Name

In the town of Rekhapur lived a tile maker named Chauka. He made square floor tiles, and he was proud of how exact they were. Every tile was one hand wide and one hand long.

One day a rich man wanted a floor with a pattern. He wanted each square tile cut in two, from corner to corner, to make triangles.

"How long is the cut?" asked Chauka's helper. "I must know, to order the right saw."

"Easy. I will measure it," said Chauka.

The line from corner to corner was longer than one hand, that was plain. It was shorter than two hands. Chauka had a fine ruler that split the hand into ten parts. The line was more than one hand and four parts. It was less than one hand and five parts.

"Somewhere between," he said. "I need a finer ruler."

He made a ruler that split the hand into a hundred parts. The line came to more than one hand and forty-one parts, and less than forty-two.

He made a ruler with a thousand parts. He needed a glass to read it. And still the end of the line fell between two marks. Never on one.

Chauka did not sleep. He went to the scholars at the school. They were sure of the answer. "Every length is some number of parts of a hand," said the oldest. "Halves, thirds, hundredths, thousandths. If your line falls between the marks, your ruler is poor or your eye is poor. Measure better."

"I have measured for a month."

"Then you are a poor measurer," said the scholar, and he closed the door.

Chauka went home very low. His daughter Karni was nine. She had watched all of it.

"Baba, maybe nothing is wrong with you. Maybe the line just does not sit on any mark."

"Every length has a number, child. The scholars say so."

"Do they know? Or do they only say?"

Chauka had no answer.

Karni sat down on the floor with four tiles. She pushed them together into one big square. Then she drew the corner-to-corner line on each tile, so that the four lines made a new square in the middle, standing on its point.

"Look. The slanted square is made of four half tiles. So it is as big as two tiles, exactly. And its side is your line. So your line is the side of a square that is exactly two tiles big."

"Yes. And what number is it?"

"I do not know," said Karni. "Maybe there is no number for it yet. But there it is. I cannot say it. I can draw it."

Chauka looked at the drawing for a long time. The line was there on the floor, as real as his own hand.

He went back to the school with the four tiles and laid them on the step. This time one young scholar did not close the door. She sat down beside the tiles and stayed there until dark.

It was many years before people found a name for that line. But it did not wait for its name. It had been there all along.

## 4.6 The Ant Road

The prince was coming. The whole town stood on the two sides of the road to see his chariot go by, with its four white horses and its golden wheels.

A girl named Cheenti stood at the front. She was not looking at the horses. She was looking at the ground.

A long line of ants was crossing the road. There were thousands of them. Each one was carrying a white egg. Their old nest had flooded in the night, and they were moving house.

The chariot came on. The wheels were as tall as a man.

Cheenti stepped into the road and held up both hands.

The horses stopped. The crowd gasped. Nobody stopped the prince.

A man in a long robe got down. He was the court logician, the cleverest man in the kingdom. It was his job to give the reason for things.

"Child, move aside. You are in the prince's way."

"Sir, the ants are crossing. They will be over soon. Please wait, or go round."

The logician smiled. He liked to teach. "Listen and learn. The prince is great. Ants are small. Small things do not matter when great things must pass. That is the rule."

Cheenti was afraid, but she was thinking. She had been taught that you can test a rule by turning it round.

"Is that the rule, sir? Small things do not matter to big things?"

"That is the rule."

"Then I do not matter to you. I am much smaller than you."

"That is why I told you to move."

"And you, sir. Are you bigger than the prince?"

The logician coughed. "No one is greater than the prince."

"So you do not matter to the prince. He may drive over you if you are in his way."

The crowd began to murmur. The logician opened his mouth and shut it again.

"And the prince," said Cheenti. She pointed far off, to where the mountain stood white against the sky. "He is smaller than the mountain. If stones roll down on the palace, will you say it is right, because small things do not matter?"

It was very quiet.

"I do not think you believe your rule, sir," said Cheenti. "Nobody wants it when he is the small one. And everyone is the small one to somebody."

A laugh came from the chariot. The prince himself got down. He was young, not much older than her brother. He walked to the line of ants and squatted to look.

"They carry their children," he said, surprised. He stood up. "I have just learned something on my own road. A rule that I would not accept from the mountain, I should not give to the ant."

He looked at the logician. "Is there a fault in her reasoning?"

"No, my prince," said the logician. He was an honest man after all. "The fault was in mine."

The prince did not get back into the chariot. He walked round the ants on foot, and the horses were led round after him through the field.

The ants did not look up. They had work to do.

## 4.7 The Stone That Chose North

Chumbi found the stone in a dry stream bed. It was black and heavy, the size of a mango. She put it in her bag with her iron needle case, and when she got home the needle case was stuck to the stone.

She pulled it off. It jumped back.

By evening the whole village was in her yard.

"It is alive," said one man. "It grabs things. Throw it in the river before it grabs a child."

"It is holy," said a woman. "We must build a little house for it and bring it flowers."

Half the village wanted to throw it away. Half wanted to bow to it. They began to shout at each other.

Chumbi held the stone tightly. "Wait. We do not even know what it does. Let us find out first. Then we can decide."

She brought things from the house, one by one, and held each one near the stone.

An iron nail. It jumped to the stone. A copper cup. Nothing. A wooden spoon. Nothing. A gold earring from her aunt. Nothing. An iron key. Jump.

"It does not grab everything. Only iron. So it will not grab a child. Children are not made of iron."

A few people laughed. The shouting got softer.

"Does it work through things?" She put a leaf between the stone and the nail. Jump. A piece of cloth. Jump. Her own hand. The nail stuck to her palm, with the stone on the other side! It felt like nothing at all.

"Does it get tired?" She hung nails from it all night. In the morning they still hung there.

Then she found the strangest thing. She tied the stone to a thread and let it hang. It turned slowly and stopped. She pushed it. It swung, turned back and stopped again, with the same end pointing the same way. Towards the mountains in the north. Every single time.

"It is not a monster and it is not a god," Chumbi told the village. "It is a stone that pulls iron and points north. I do not know why. But that is what it does, and it does the same thing every time."

That summer a man came to the fair, selling "wishing stones". He would close his eyes, and say strange words, and an iron ring would slide across his table by itself to whoever paid him. People gave him their grain for a wish.

Chumbi watched him for one morning. Then she said, loud and clear, "Sir, please do it once more, on this table of mine."

He would not. So she walked round and lifted his tablecloth. Under the table he was holding a black stone, just like hers, and moving it with his knee.

"It pulls iron," she said. "Through wood too. It does not hear wishes."

The man left before noon.

Chumbi put her stone to work. Before the summer was over it had found eleven lost needles in the dust, two fish hooks and the key to the grain store. And when herders went out on cloudy days, they came to borrow the stone on its thread, to know which way was north.

## 4.8 The Loudest Voice

The village of Koop needed a new well. The old one was going dry.

In Koop, things were decided at the assembly, under the big banyan tree. Everyone could speak. And the side that shouted loudest won. That was how it had always been.

"Dig by the market road!" shouted Garaj. He had a voice like a drum. His friends shouted with him. "By the road! By the road!"

A girl named Dheemi stood at the back. All summer she had been walking the land with her goats. She had seen that in one low place, behind the potter's house, the grass stayed green when everything else was brown. Frogs lived there. In the early morning the ground was damp.

"The water is behind the potter's house," she said. But she had a small voice. Nobody heard.

She tried again, louder. Garaj's friends shouted louder still.

Dheemi went home angry. Then she sat down and thought. "Shouting shows who has a big chest. It does not show how many people agree. And it does not show who is right. Those are three different things."

The next day she went to the oldest woman in the assembly and said, "Grandmother, if ten quiet people want one thing and three loud people want another, which side wins?"

"The loud side."

"Is that fair?"

The old woman thought. "No. But how else would we know what people want?"

"Give everyone one pebble. Two pots. Each person drops their pebble in one pot. Then we count."

The assembly liked the idea. Even Garaj agreed, for he was sure he would win anyway.

They counted. Twenty-six pebbles for the road. Nine pebbles for the potter's house.

Dheemi had lost. And this time it was fair. Everyone had been counted once.

It was hard. She wanted to say that the count was wrong. But it was not wrong. She took a breath.

"The count is fair and I accept it," she said. "But the count tells us what most people think. It does not tell us where the water is. The water does not know about pebbles. May I ask for one small thing? Before we dig the big well, let us dig two small holes, as deep as my arm. One by the road and one behind the potter's house. It will take one morning. Then let us vote again."

People looked at each other. One morning was not much.

They dug by the road. Dry dust, all the way down.

They dug behind the potter's house. At the depth of an elbow, the earth turned dark. At the depth of an arm, water began to seep into the hole.

Nobody shouted. They voted again. Thirty-four pebbles for the potter's house, and one for the road.

"Who was the one?" asked Dheemi.

"Me," said Garaj, a little red. "I do not like to change my mind in one day." Then he picked up a spade. "But I can dig."

Koop still decides with pebbles. And when the question is about what is so, and not about what people want, they dig a small hole first.

## 4.9 The Girl Who Could Not Run Out of Sentences

Vani talked. She talked when she woke up and she talked when she ate. She talked to the goats, the pots and the Moon.

One night she talked in her sleep, and when she opened her eyes she was in a great grey hall. On a grey chair sat a tall man with no mouth that she could see. Yet she heard him.

"I am the Silence King. Every person is given a bag of sentences at birth. You have used nearly all of yours. Seven are left. After that you will be silent for ever, and you will live here with me."

Beside him, in a cage, sat her own parrot, Mitthu, who had gone missing last week. So this was where things went when they stopped making noise.

Vani opened her mouth and shut it again. Seven!

"That cannot be true," she said. One gone.

"Sentences are made of words, and there are only so many words. So there are only so many sentences. All the sentences have been said before, by somebody. You have no new ones."

Vani thought hard, without speaking, which was new for her.

Her teacher had just begun to teach the class about how sentences are built. A naming part, a doing part. And small joining words, like hooks, that tie one sentence to the next.

"If I say a sentence that nobody has ever said before, will you let us go?" Two gone.

The Silence King made a sound like dry leaves. "There is no such sentence. But yes. Say one, and you and the bird are free."

"My parrot is green."

"Said ten thousand times."

"My parrot is green and my goat is brown."

"Said before, by a boy in the hills."

Five gone. Two left. Vani's heart was beating hard. Then she saw it. The hook. The little word "and". You could hang anything on it. And then hang something more on that.

She took the biggest breath of her life.

"My parrot is green and my goat is brown and I talked in my sleep and I came to a grey hall and I met a king with no mouth and he keeps a cage and he says that sentences run out and he is wrong and today my mother made rice and I spilled it and the Moon was thin and..."

"Stop," said the Silence King.

"...and I can go on, because after every 'and' I may put any sentence at all, and after that another 'and', and it never has to end. Has anybody ever said that whole thing before?"

The grey hall was quiet.

"No," said the Silence King at last. "Nobody. And you could make another, and another." The dry-leaf sound came again, and Vani saw that it was laughter. "A few words. A few rules. And no end to what can be said. I had not seen it."

He opened the cage. Mitthu flew to her shoulder.

"You have no bag," said the King. "No one has. Speech does not run out. So there is no need to pour it all out at once. Go home. And since you may say anything, choose what is true and what is kind."

Vani woke in her bed. Mitthu was on the window sill.

She had one sentence left over from the seven, and she used it. "Thank you," she said.

## 4.10 Long, Short, Long

Three friends lived in three houses that shared walls: Taal, Maatra and Laghu, who was in the middle.

One winter Laghu caught a fever with spots. The healer said, "He will be well in three weeks. But until then nobody may go into his room, or they will catch it too."

Three weeks alone! On the first day Taal and Maatra stood in the lane and shouted through his window. On the second day the healer said shouting made him cough.

On the third day Taal knocked on the wall, just to say hello. And from the other side came a knock back.

"We can knock to him!" said Taal.

"But a knock is only a knock," said Maatra. "It says 'I am here', and nothing more."

"What if one knock means 'hello' and two knocks mean 'are you well'?"

"And for 'are you hungry'? A hundred knocks? We need more signals without more knocking."

Maatra was learning to play the drum. Her teacher had taught her that a beat can be short, tak, or long, taaa.

"Two kinds of knock," she said. "A short one with the knuckle. A long one, a slow drag with the palm. Now, with one knock we have two signals: short, or long."

"And with two knocks?" said Taal. He drew in the dust. "Short-short. Short-long. Long-short. Long-long. Four!"

"And with three?"

They wrote them all out. Every signal of two knocks could have a short added in front of it, or a long. So four became eight.

Short-short-short. Short-short-long. Short-long-short. Short-long-long. Long-short-short. Long-short-long. Long-long-short. Long-long-long.

"Eight signals from only three knocks!" said Taal. "And four knocks would give sixteen. It doubles every time."

They chose eight meanings. Good morning. Good night. Are you well? Yes. No. I am bored. Wait. And long-short-long, the best one: We are here.

They wrote the list on a leaf and sent it in with his food. That evening they knocked: Are you well?

A pause. Then, slowly, from the other side: short-short-long. Yes.

They nearly cried with joy.

For two weeks the wall talked. Good morning. I am bored. We are here. Laghu's mother said he smiled every time he heard it.

Then one evening, after Good night, something new came through the wall.

Long-long. Short-short. Long-long.

"Six knocks?" said Taal. "That is not on our list."

"He made a mistake. The fever."

It came again, just the same. Long-long, short-short, long-long.

Maatra clapped it softly with her hands. Taaa-taaa, tak-tak, taaa-taaa. "It is not a mistake. It is the beat of the harvest dance. The one we three do together." Her eyes were shining. "He is saying, 'I am getting better and I want to dance.' He has made a new word. One that we did not give him."

"So the list is not finished," said Taal slowly. "We can keep making words."

By the time Laghu came out, thin and smiling, the wall had forty words. They never stopped using it, even when he was well.

## 4.11 The Ship That Sank Without Sinking

Laher and her little brother Boond lived by the sea. One morning their father's ship sailed for a far port. They stood on the sand to watch it go.

The ship grew smaller and smaller. That was all right. Far things look small. They knew that.

But then something happened that was not all right.

The bottom of the ship began to vanish. First the dark body of the ship was cut off by the line of the sea. Then only the sail stood on the water. Then only the top of the sail. Then nothing.

"It sank!" screamed Boond. "It went down into the water! Baba's ship sank!"

Laher's stomach turned over. It had looked just like sinking. The body first, then the sail.

They ran along the shore to the old sailor, Samudra, who sat mending nets.

"Our father's ship sank! We saw it go down!"

Samudra did not jump up. He put down his net. "Tell me what you saw. Slowly. Just what you saw."

"The ship went far. Then the bottom went under, then the sail, then the top."

"Did it tip? Did it lean? Was there a storm?"

"No," said Laher. "It stood straight. The sea was flat."

"Do ships sink standing straight, on a flat sea, in fair weather?"

Laher thought. "No."

"Then let us not say 'sank' yet. You saw the bottom vanish first. That is so. Now I will ask you one thing. A ship is coming home this afternoon, from the same far port. When it first shows, which part will you see first? Think, do not guess."

Boond sniffed. "All of it, very small. A tiny dot of a ship."

Laher was quiet. "If Baba's ship had truly sunk, that tells me nothing about a ship coming home. But what if it did not sink? What if something was in the way and hid it? The bottom hid first, so the thing in the way was low. What is between us and a ship far out? Only the sea." She frowned. "Can the sea be in the way of the sea?"

"Can a hill be in the way of a man who walks over it?" asked Samudra.

"Yes. When he goes down the far side, his feet go first, then his body, then his head." Laher's eyes grew wide. "Is the sea like a hill? Is it round?"

"I am asking you what you will see first this afternoon."

"The top of the sail," said Laher. "If the sea is curved like a hill, a ship coming home will climb up over it. Head first. Sail first."

They waited on the sand all afternoon. Boond held her hand.

Far out, on the line where the sea meets the sky, a small white point came up. Just a point. Then it was a sail, with nothing under it. Then, slowly, the dark body of the ship rose up below the sail, as if it were climbing out of the water.

"Sail first!" shouted Boond.

"The sea is round," said Laher. "The whole Earth must be round. And Baba did not sink. He only went over the hill."

Twenty days later their father's ship came home. They saw the top of the sail first.

## 4.12 The Brother Who Could Not Be Caught

Gati was a quick runner. Her little brother Mand was four, and he ran like a duck.

One evening a traveller came to the village. He was a talker. He could make words stand on their heads. People gave him food just to listen.

He saw Gati chasing Mand round the yard, and he smiled.

"Girl, you will never catch him."

Gati laughed. "I catch him every day."

"Not if he starts ahead of you. I can prove it. Listen. Mand stands at the tree. You stand at the gate, far behind. You both start to run. Now, before you can catch him, you must first reach the tree, where he was. True?"

"True."

"But by then he has moved on. A little way, for he is slow. He is at the well. So now you must reach the well. True?"

"True."

"But by then he has moved on again. A smaller way. He is at the stone. You reach the stone. He has moved on again, a tiny way. Every time you reach where he was, he is no longer there. There is always one more gap to close. One more, and one more, with no end. And a thing with no end can never be finished. So you can never catch him."

The villagers looked at each other. Every step of it sounded right. Some of them nodded slowly. One old man said, "Then how did anybody ever catch anybody?"

Gati went over the steps in her head. She could not find the wrong one. Each step was true. But the end was not.

"I cannot find the mistake in your words," she said. "But I can still test them. Mand, go to the tree."

Mand stood at the tree. Gati stood at the gate.

"Run!"

Mand ran like a duck. Gati ran like the wind. She passed the tree. She passed the well. Just beyond the stone she picked him up and swung him round. He screamed with joy.

"I caught him," she said, hardly out of breath. "So your proof is wrong somewhere. I do not know where yet. But a proof that says a thing cannot happen is wrong, if the thing happens."

The traveller clapped. "Good! Most people believe my words more than their own eyes. But can you find the fault? I shall be here until morning."

Gati lay awake. She went through the run again in her mind.

To reach the tree took her, say, ten breaths. To reach the well, one breath more. To reach the stone, only a small part of a breath. The next gap, a smaller part still.

"The gaps do go on for ever," she thought. "He is right about that. There is no last one. But each one takes less time than the one before. Much less. Ten breaths, one breath, a bit of a breath, a bit of a bit."

She sat up.

"He counted the steps. He should have added up the time. There are endless steps, but they are so small that all of them together fit inside eleven or twelve breaths. Endless pieces can make a small whole. Like cutting one roti in half, and half again, and half again. Endless cuts, but still just one roti."

In the morning she told the traveller.

He bowed to her, all the way down. "You did both things," he said. "You ran the race, and then you stayed up to find out why. That is all there is to it."
