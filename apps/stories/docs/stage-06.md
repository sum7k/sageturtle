## 6.1 Shunya Comes to Number Street

Number Street had nine houses, and they stood in order of size.

At the low end lived One, in a hut with a single window. Next came Two, then Three, and so on up the hill. At the very top, in a house with nine pillars, lived Nine. Nine had the most of everything, and he made sure that everybody knew it.

One morning a stranger walked up the street. He was perfectly round, like a ring, and there was nothing inside him at all. You could see the sky through him.

"Who are you?" asked the digits.

"I am Shunya. Some call me Zero. May I live here?"

"What are you worth?" asked Nine. On Number Street that was always the first question.

"By myself? Nothing."

The digits laughed until their roofs shook. "Nothing! Then you are below One. Below the lowest!"

"There is no house below One," said Seven.

"Then build him a hut at the bottom, behind the well," said Nine. "A number worth nothing can live where nobody looks."

So Shunya lived behind the well. He did not complain. He was a calm sort of number. But the young digits were curious, and they came to look at him. The most curious was little One. Being the smallest on the street, she knew how it felt.

"What can you do?" she asked.

"Let us find out," said Shunya. "Add me to yourself."

One added Zero. She looked down at herself. "I am still One. You did nothing!"

"Yes. When I am added, nothing changes. I take nothing and I give nothing. Not everyone can say that."

"That is not much of a trick."

"No. Here is another. Come and stand beside me. You on the left. I on the right."

One stood beside him.

A shiver went down Number Street. Up the hill, Nine dropped his cup. Where little One had been standing, there now stood Ten. One and Zero side by side made a number bigger than Nine himself.

"But you are worth nothing!" cried One. "How did you make me ten times as big?"

"I did not add anything to you," said Shunya. "I only held a place. When I stand in the ones' place, you are pushed up into the tens' place. You are still a one. But now you are one ten. An empty place can do that."

The news ran up the street. Two came down the hill and stood by Shunya: Twenty. Five came: Fifty. Then One came back and brought Shunya's reflection in the well, and they stood three in a row: One Hundred.

Nine watched from his nine pillars, and he did not like it. For the first time, he was not the biggest. But Nine was clever, and Nine was greedy.

That evening he came down to the hut behind the well with a heavy bag.

"Shunya, my friend. I have judged you too quickly. I hear that you make numbers bigger. I have here nine gold coins. Stand beside them."

"I can do that," said Shunya. "Nine and I, side by side, make ninety."

"Ninety. Hm. But I have heard of a stronger way than standing beside. One and Two were talking about it. It is called multiplying. Three times two is six. Three times three is nine. It makes things grow fast. So. If standing beside you gives me ninety, then multiplying by you must give me even more. Multiply my coins by yourself. Now."

"I would not," said Shunya. "Nine, listen. Multiplying means 'so many lots of'. Three times your coins is three lots of nine. But I am zero. Zero times your coins is no lots of nine. It is..."

"Do not teach me arithmetic! I was the greatest number on this street before you were born. Do it."

"Nine, please think first."

"Do it!"

Shunya sighed, and he did it.

Nine opened his bag. He turned it upside down. He shook it. Not one coin fell out. No lots of nine is nothing.

"Thief!" screamed Nine. "Give them back!"

"I did not take them," said Shunya sadly. "I told you. Whatever is multiplied by me becomes me. It is not a thing that I do out of spite. It is what I am. A thousand times zero, a million times zero. All zero. I am the gentlest number when I am added, and I am the most dangerous when I am multiplied. That is why you should have listened."

The whole street had gathered. Nine stood with his empty bag. Nobody laughed, for they could all see that it might have been any of them.

Little One spoke up. "Shunya, can you undo it?"

"No. From nothing, I cannot tell what was there before. Nine times zero is zero. Five times zero is zero. Once it is done, the zero does not remember which it was."

Nine sat down on the edge of the well. He was quiet for a long time. Then, in a small voice for so big a number, he said, "I was the one who would not listen. Not you."

The street took up a collection that night. Each digit gave Nine one coin. It came to eight, since Nine could hardly give to himself. Then Shunya came and added himself, which made no difference at all, and everybody laughed, even Nine.

After that the digits trusted Shunya. They came to him with their sums. And so they were surprised on the day when he said no.

It was Six who asked. Six had found six ripe mangoes and wanted to practise dividing.

"Six mangoes among three friends: two each," said Six. "Among two friends: three each. Among one: he gets all six. Now, Shunya. Divide my six mangoes among zero friends. How many does each one get?"

Shunya was silent.

"Well?"

"I will not answer that," said Shunya.

"You cannot?"

"Listen. If I say 'each gets six', then who has them? There is nobody there. If I say 'each gets zero', then where did the mangoes go? You still have six in your hands. If I say 'each gets a million', it is just as true and just as false. There is no 'each'. The question asks about people who are not there. Any answer I gave would be a lie. And I may be worth nothing, but I do not lie."

The digits thought about that.

"So there is one thing that even you will not do," said little One.

"One thing," said Shunya. "I will add. I will hold a place. I will even multiply, if you are sure. But do not ask me to divide by myself. Some questions have no true answer. The honest thing is to say so."

They moved Shunya out from behind the well. He did not want a grand house. So they built him a small round one, at the very beginning of the street, before One. From there he could see every number that came after him. And every one of them, sooner or later, came to stand beside him.

## 6.2 The Stranger Called X

On the last day of the autumn fair, a visitor came to the town of Tula. He wore a long cloak and a mask with a cross painted on it, like two sticks laid over each other.

He stopped in front of the balance keeper's stall. The balance keeper was an old woman named Barabar. Her great brass scales settled every argument in the market.

"I am a number," said the stranger. "My name for today is X. Whoever finds out which number I am shall have a silver coin. But I will not tell you straight. I will only tell you things about myself."

A crowd gathered. Children pushed to the front, and among them was a girl named Samaa.

"The first one is easy," said X. "Three of me, and four more, make nineteen."

People began to shout guesses. "Seven!" "Four!" "Six!"

"Wait," said Barabar. "Guessing is slow. Let us weigh it. Samaa, help me."

She put a row of small bags on the table. "Imagine that each of these bags holds X pebbles. We do not know how many. On the left pan I put three bags, and four loose pebbles. On the right pan I put nineteen loose pebbles. He says they are equal. So the scales are level."

She held up one finger. "There is only one rule in my stall. Whatever you do to one pan, do the same to the other. Then the scales stay level, and what was true stays true. Samaa, what would you like to do?"

Samaa looked at the scales. "The four loose pebbles on the left are in the way. I want the bags alone. Take four pebbles off the left."

"And?"

"And four off the right. That is the rule." She counted. "Now it is three bags on the left, and fifteen pebbles on the right."

"Still level. What next?"

"Three bags weigh fifteen. I want one bag. If I take a third of the left, I must take a third of the right. One bag. Five pebbles." She looked up. "You are five!"

"Check it," said Barabar. She always said that.

"Three fives are fifteen. And four is nineteen. Yes."

X bowed and put a silver coin on the table. "I am five. For that riddle. Here is a harder one. Twice me, less three, is the same as me and four more."

The crowd groaned. "He is on both sides now!"

"That makes no difference to the scales," said Barabar. "Samaa?"

Samaa thought it through aloud. "Left pan: two bags, with three pebbles taken out. Right pan: one bag and four pebbles. First I will mend the left. It is short by three. So I add three pebbles to the left, and three to the right. Now it is two bags on the left. One bag and seven pebbles on the right."

"Still level."

"Now there is a bag on each side. I take one bag off the left and one bag off the right. I do not know what a bag weighs. But I know they weigh the same, so the scales stay level." She smiled. "One bag on the left. Seven pebbles on the right. You are seven!"

"Check."

"Twice seven is fourteen. Less three is eleven. Seven and four is eleven. Yes."

"But you were five!" a boy shouted at the stranger. "How can you be seven now?"

"I am whatever number makes the riddle true," said X. "No more, no less. In each riddle I am hiding in a different place."

He put down a second coin. Then he leaned forward, and his voice dropped.

"The last one. Listen well. If you multiply me by myself, you get the same as five of me, less six."

There was silence. Nobody knew how to put "me times me" in a bag.

Samaa frowned. "I cannot weigh this one step by step. Not yet. But I can still test. If you were one: one times one is one. Five ones less six is... less than nothing. No. If you were two: two times two is four. Five twos are ten, less six is four. Four and four! You are two!"

"Check it," said Barabar. But she was looking at the stranger, and her eyes were narrow.

"I have checked it. It is two." Samaa held out her hand for the coin.

"Is that all of the checking?" said Barabar softly. "You stopped at the first number that fitted. Did the riddle say that there is only one?"

Samaa's hand dropped. She had not thought of that. Every riddle so far had had one answer.

"If you were three," she said slowly. "Three times three is nine. Five threes are fifteen, less six is... nine." She stared. "Three works too. You are two. And you are three. Both are true. But you cannot be two numbers!"

"Can I not?" said X.

He lifted his hands and pulled off the mask and threw back the cloak.

Under the cloak were two small people, one standing on the other's shoulders. The one on top was a boy, with a big 3 on his shirt. The one beneath was his twin sister, with a 2.

The crowd roared with laughter.

"Some riddles have one of us in them," said the girl called Two, rubbing her shoulders.

"And some have both," said the boy called Three, jumping down. "When a number is multiplied by itself, watch out. There may be two of us under the cloak."

They put two silver coins on the table. One for each answer.

Samaa took them. She gave one back to Barabar. "That one is yours. I would have stopped at two."

"What will you remember?" asked the old woman.

"Three things," said Samaa. "Whatever I do to one side, I do to the other. Then, always check. And when I have found an answer, I should ask whether it is the only one."

## 6.3 The Council in the Pot

The village of Kudavur had a way of choosing its council that people from other places found very odd.

They did not choose the strongest. They did not choose the richest. They did not shout, and they did not even vote. Every year, each ward of the village wrote the names of its fit people on palm leaves. Fit meant that you owned a little land, could read, were honest in your accounts and had not served in the last three years. Each name was written on one leaf. All the leaves of a ward went into a pot. The pot was shaken. Then, in front of the whole village, a small child put in a hand and drew out one leaf.

That person served for a year. That was all.

"But why leave it to chance?" a traveller once asked. "Surely you want the best person."

The oldest woman of Kudavur answered. "Who is to say who is best? Everyone thinks it is himself or his cousin. When we used to choose, the same three families chose each other every year. With the pot, every fit person has the same chance. Nobody can buy it and nobody can frighten it. The pot has no cousins."

In the year of this story, the child chosen to draw was a girl of eight named Olai. She had been chosen because she was too young to have any friends or enemies among the grown-ups. She was proud and nervous. She practised by picking tamarind seeds out of a jar with her eyes shut.

There was a man in the fourth ward named Perumai. He was the richest man in Kudavur. He had wanted to sit on the council for ten years. He wanted it badly, for the council decided who got water from the tank, and when. The pot had never once picked him.

"One leaf among thirty," he grumbled. "I could wait thirty years. A man of my standing, waiting on the fingers of a child!"

That year, Perumai did something that he had never done before. On the night before the drawing, the pot for the fourth ward stood sealed in the meeting hall. The watchman liked his sleep. Perumai came in the dark with a bundle of leaves. On each one he had written his own name. Twenty of them. He lifted the cloth and slid them in.

Now there were fifty leaves in the pot, and twenty-one of them said Perumai.

Morning came. The village gathered under the great tree. The pot was carried out. The elder untied the cloth and shook the pot, and held it low for Olai.

Olai shut her eyes, as she had practised, and put in her hand.

Her fingers moved among the leaves. Then they stopped.

When she had practised with tamarind seeds, they had all felt different. These leaves were written by thirty different hands in thirty different homes, and they should feel different too. Some would be old and stiff and some fresh. Some would be cut wide and some narrow. But her fingers kept finding leaves that were exactly alike. The same width. The same fresh smoothness. The same cut corner. One after another after another.

She opened her eyes. She did not draw. She said, "Something is wrong."

There was a murmur. "Draw, child," said the elder.

Olai was frightened. It would have been so easy to pull out a leaf and sit down. But she had been told, when they chose her, "You are the hand of the whole village. Your hand must be honest."

"I am sorry. I cannot draw yet. Many of the leaves feel the same. As if one person had made them all. Please may we look?"

The elder frowned. Then he tipped the pot out on a white cloth, in front of everyone.

Fifty leaves. The ward had thirty fit people.

They were sorted by name. Twenty-nine names had one leaf each. One name had twenty-one. And twenty of those were cut from the same branch and written in the same thick, proud hand.

Nobody needed to ask whose.

Perumai stood up. His face was dark red. He could have shouted. He could have blamed a servant. But two hundred people were looking at him, and at twenty leaves in his own handwriting.

"I did it," he said. "I wanted it too much. I am ashamed."

The village was quiet. Then people began to talk all at once. Some wanted him fined. Some wanted his name struck from the pot for ever.

The oldest woman raised her hand. "First, the rule. Then the man. How did this happen? Because the pot sat alone in the dark, and we trusted a cloth and a sleepy watchman. A rule that works only if nobody is watching is a weak rule. Let us mend the rule so that it does not depend on anyone being good."

Olai, still standing by the pot, said, "Could we count the leaves? Out loud, as they go in? So that everyone hears thirty, and no more."

"In the daylight," said someone.

"And read each name aloud as it goes in," said another. "Then anyone can say, 'That name has gone in already.'"

"And shake it and draw at once. No night in between."

So it was settled. From that day in Kudavur, the leaves went into the pot in front of the whole village. They were read aloud, one by one, and counted by all. Then the pot was shaken and the child drew straight away. Nobody had to trust anybody. They had only to watch.

They did it again that same morning for the fourth ward. Thirty leaves. Olai drew. The name was that of a quiet potter, who nearly fell over.

As for Perumai, he was fined a year's water tax. He paid it without a word. The council decided, after a long talk, that his name could go into the pot again after three years. "One leaf," said the oldest woman. "Like everyone else. He has not lost the right to be equal. He never had the right to be more than equal."

It is said that five years later, the child drew a leaf, and the elder read out, "Perumai."

The village held its breath. One leaf among thirty. Drawn fairly, in daylight, in front of everybody.

Perumai served his year. People say that he was careful, fair and almost painfully honest with the water, and that he never once asked for a second term.

"I spent ten years trying to get that seat," he told Olai, who was thirteen by then. "The only way that I could ever sit in it with my head up was the way that I got it at last. By one leaf, the same as the potter."

## 6.4 The Hole in the Shutter

In the city of Noor there were two famous scholars, and they had been arguing for nineteen years.

The question was this: how do we see?

The first scholar, Kiran, said: "Sight goes out from the eye. The eye sends out fine rays, like the feelers of an insect. They touch a thing, and so we see it. That is why we must turn our eyes towards a thing to see it. That is why we see in straight lines."

The second scholar, Roshan, said: "Nonsense. Sight comes in. Every object sends tiny copies of itself flying through the air, like skins from a snake. They enter the eye whole. That is why a strong light hurts the eye. Something has come in."

Each had written eleven books. Each had students who would not speak to the other's students. Every year they held a great debate in the city hall, and every year the one who shouted more cleverly was said to have won. Next year it would go the other way.

A girl named Najar swept the floor of the hall where they debated. She was twelve. She had listened to four of these debates, leaning on her broom.

"They are both very clever," she said to her uncle, who was the doorkeeper. "But they only talk. How will talking ever settle it? If two people argue about whether a pot is full, somebody should lift the lid."

"And how do you lift the lid on seeing?"

Najar did not know. But she kept the question.

One hot afternoon she was lying in the storeroom behind the hall. The room had no window. It had only a wooden shutter, closed tight against the heat, and the room was as dark as night. She was half asleep.

On the white wall opposite the shutter, something was moving.

She sat up. There was a faint picture on the wall. A patch of pale blue at the bottom. Green shapes hanging down from the top. And little dark shapes that crossed slowly from one side to the other.

She went close. Her heart was beating hard. The green shapes were trees, upside down. The blue at the bottom was the sky. The little dark shapes were people in the street outside, walking on their heads.

She turned round. In the shutter was one small knot hole, no bigger than a pea. A thread of light came through it.

She put her finger over the hole. The picture vanished. She took her finger away. It came back.

"The street is coming in through the hole," she whispered. "By itself. I am not looking at the street. My eyes are turned to the wall. Nothing from my eyes is going out there. The light is coming in, and it is bringing the picture."

But why upside down?

She thought about it for a long time. She held a stick across the hole and watched. Light from the top of a tree came down through the hole and went on, in a straight line, to land low on the wall. Light from the ground went up through the hole and landed high. The lines crossed at the hole, like the two blades of a pair of scissors.

"Light goes straight," she said. "It does not bend round to keep things the right way up. It goes straight through the hole, and so top and bottom change places."

She ran to fetch her uncle. He looked, and sat down on a sack.

It happened that a search was going on in the street that very day. The baker's goat had got loose that morning. It was a valuable white one, and half the lane was hunting for it. As Najar and her uncle watched the wall, a small pale shape moved along the bottom edge of the picture, which was the top of the world. It was up on something.

"That is a goat," said Najar. "Upside down, so it is really up high. On the roof of the dyer's shed. Nobody has looked up."

Her uncle went out. The goat was on the dyer's roof, eating his drying cloth.

Word reached the scholars by evening. To their credit, both came. They would not stand near each other, but both crowded into the dark storeroom. Najar closed the door.

The street appeared on the wall.

Neither man spoke for a full minute.

"The picture comes in," said Roshan at last, in triumph. "As I have said for nineteen years! Things send their copies into the eye!"

"Sir," said Najar, "may I ask something? If whole copies fly off things, like skins, why does the picture need the tiny hole? Open the shutter wide."

She opened it. Light flooded in. The wall was only a bright wall. No picture.

"With a big opening, there is more light but no picture. If whole copies of the tree came sailing in, a big window should let in a better picture. It lets in none. And why is it upside down? A copy would not turn itself over. I think it is not copies. It is only light, going in straight lines from every point of the tree. The small hole lets just one thin line through from each point. So they land in order, and they cross on the way."

Roshan opened his mouth and closed it.

Kiran smiled in the dark. "So. Straight lines. As I have always said. Sight travels in straight lines."

"Yes, sir. You were right about the straight lines. But they do not go out from the eye. There is no eye here. A wall cannot send out feelers. It is light coming in."

Now neither of them was smiling.

"Half right," said Roshan slowly. "It comes in. But not as I said."

"Half right," said Kiran. "Straight lines. But not from where I said."

"Nineteen years," said Roshan.

"We could have closed a shutter," said Kiran.

They looked at each other properly for the first time in nineteen years, by the light of an upside-down street.

There was no debate that year. Instead, the two scholars and a girl with a broom spent the season in the storeroom. They tried holes of different sizes, and walls at different distances, and candles, and coloured glass. They wrote down what happened each time, whether or not it suited anyone's book.

Kiran wrote on the first page of their notes: "We argued about what must be so. A child looked at what is so."

Najar made him cross out "a child" and write her name.

## 6.5 The Needle That Seemed to Lie

The ship Sagar-Rani was eleven days out, carrying pepper and cotton across the open sea, when the fog came down.

It was the thickest fog that the crew had ever seen. They could not see the top of the mast from the deck. There was no Sun by day and no stars by night. The sea was flat and grey on every side, and every side looked the same.

Captain Disha had sailed for thirty years. She stood at the bow, sniffed the wind and felt the swell under her feet.

"Land is that way," she said, and pointed. "I can feel it. Steer so."

But the ship carried something new that year. In a wooden box near the helm, there was a bowl of water. On the water floated a sliver of iron fixed to a piece of cork. It had been rubbed on a lodestone, and it always swung to point north and south. The merchant who owned the cargo had bought it at great cost from sailors of the far east.

The mate looked into the bowl. "Captain, the needle says that north is there. So land, which lies to the east, is that way." He pointed. His arm and the captain's arm made a wide angle, wide enough to drive a cart through.

"The needle is a toy," said the captain. "I have thirty years in my bones."

"The needle has been right for eleven days," said the mate.

The crew split. The older sailors stood with the captain. "She has never lost a ship." The younger ones stood with the mate. "The needle does not have feelings. It just points."

They had water for eight days. If they steered wrong, they would sail out into the empty ocean.

The youngest person on the ship was the cabin girl, Kaanta, who was twelve. Her work was to carry food and to keep the bowl of the needle filled with water. Nobody asked what she thought. But she had spent more hours looking at that needle than anyone else on board.

She was troubled. She trusted the needle. For eleven days it had agreed with the Sun at noon and with the Pole Star at night, every time. That was real testing, day after day. The captain's feeling, as far as Kaanta knew, had never been tested in fog at all.

"A thing that has been checked many times against the sky," she thought, "is worth more than a feeling, even a wise person's feeling. In fog, everybody's sense of direction goes round in circles. I have heard the old sailors say so themselves."

And yet something nagged at her. Yesterday morning, before the fog, she had filled the bowl as usual. The needle had pointed straight along the deck plank that she used as her mark. This morning it pointed two planks to the left of her mark.

The ship had not turned. She knew that from the wind on her cheek and from the steersman, who had held the helm steady. So why had the needle moved?

"Either north has moved, or something has moved the needle," she thought. "North does not move."

She did not say anything yet. A cabin girl who cries "the needle is wrong" in the middle of that quarrel would only pour oil on the fire. First she wanted to know.

What moves a needle? She thought back to what the merchant had said when he brought it on board. "It is a lodestone's child. It loves iron. Keep iron away from it."

She looked round the box. No knives. No nails nearby, for the box was pegged with wood. The helm was wood.

Then she saw it. Against the wall, an arm's length from the needle box, sat the cook's great iron cooking pot, turned upside down.

It had not been there two days ago. Kaanta remembered now. Yesterday at noon a wave had splashed into the galley. The cook had carried his big pot up on deck to dry, and he had set it down in the nearest sheltered corner. He had left it there when the fog came.

She fetched the cook. "Uncle, may I move your pot? Only for a moment. I want to see something."

"Move it to the Moon, child. In this fog I cannot even find my onions."

The pot was heavy. She dragged it slowly along the deck, away from the box. And she watched the bowl.

The needle trembled. As the pot moved away, the needle swung. One plank. Two planks. It settled, quivering, straight along her mark.

She dragged the pot back. The needle swung left again, towards the pot, like a dog turning its head towards its dinner.

Now she was sure. She went to the mate and the captain, who were not speaking to each other.

"Captain. Sir. May I show you something? It will take as long as it takes to drink a cup of water."

She showed them. Pot near: needle swings. Pot far: needle swings back. She did it three times.

The mate went pale. "Then the needle has been wrong since yesterday. I have been steering by a needle that was looking at a cooking pot."

"You see?" said the captain. "A toy."

"No, Captain," said Kaanta, as respectfully as she could. Her mouth was dry. "The needle was not lying. It did just what it always does. It points to the strongest pull. Far from iron, that is north. With a great lump of iron beside it, it is partly the iron. We fooled it. It is like a good witness who has been made to stand in the wrong place. Now the pot is gone. Look where it points."

With the pot far forward, the needle sat steady. The mate drew the line of east from it, and they all looked along his arm.

It was not where the mate had pointed that morning. And it was not where the captain had pointed. It lay between the two, though nearer to the mate's.

The captain looked at the needle for a long time. She was a proud woman. But she had not lost a ship in thirty years, and that was not from pride.

"Thirty years in my bones," she said. "And my bones were off by a cart's width. Mate, your needle was off too. Neither of us was right. The girl is the only one who asked why the two did not agree." She turned to Kaanta. "What made you look?"

"The needle had been tested against the Sun and the stars for eleven days, Captain. So I trusted it more than a feeling. But yesterday it changed when the ship had not. A good tool is still a tool. If I know what it loves, I know what can fool it."

"Steer by the needle," said the captain. "And cook! Your pot lives at the bow from now on. Paint a line on the deck round that box, three paces out. No iron crosses the line. Not a pot, not a knife, not a belt buckle."

They steered by the needle for two days through the grey. On the third morning the fog lifted like a curtain. There, straight off the bow, was the low green line of the coast.

The captain put the cabin girl in charge of the needle box for the rest of the voyage. Kaanta drew the three-pace line herself. The cook was heard to grumble that he had been sailing for twenty years and it was the first time that his cooking pot had nearly drowned anyone.

## 6.6 The Dancer in the Mould

In the bronze workers' lane of a town in the south, a girl named Mezhugu was learning to cast.

Her teacher was the master Sthapati, whose bronzes stood in the halls of kings. He had taken her as an apprentice when she was nine. For three years she had done nothing but knead wax and beat clay. Now she was twelve, and for the first time he said, "Make a figure. Your own."

She took a lump of beeswax, softened with tree resin. For two months she shaped it with her fingers and with small tools of horn and wood.

She made a dancer. A girl standing on one foot, with the other knee lifted and one arm flung out. Her head was tilted as if she had just heard the drum. It was no taller than Mezhugu's forearm. Every finger was there. The folds of the skirt seemed to swing. There was the beginning of a smile.

It was the best thing that she had ever made. She knew it. The other apprentices came to look and were silent, which is the highest praise that apprentices give.

"Good," said the master. From him that was a great deal. "Now we cast her."

Mezhugu knew the way of casting. She had helped with it a hundred times for other people's work. But she had never truly thought about it until this moment.

This was how it went. You took your wax figure. You coated it with fine wet clay, very gently, so that the clay pressed into every line. Then more clay, coarser. Then more, until the figure was buried inside a thick, rough lump, with a small hole left at the bottom. You dried the lump. Then you put it in the fire.

In the fire, the wax melted. It ran out of the hole and was gone. You were left with a baked clay lump, with an empty space inside it in the exact shape of your figure. Into that space you poured the molten bronze. When it had cooled, you broke the clay, and there was your figure in metal.

Wax in. Wax out. Bronze in.

"The wax must be lost," said Mezhugu slowly.

"That is why it is called lost wax," said the master.

"But this is my dancer. If she melts, she is gone. If the bronze does not flow well, or the mould cracks, I will have nothing. No wax and no bronze. I can never make her just the same again. My hands will not do it twice."

"That is so."

"Can we not keep her in wax?"

"We can. Put her on a shelf. In the hot season she will sag. In two years, mice will have eaten her fingers. In five she will be a grey lump. Wax does not last, child. It is only the way to the thing that lasts."

Mezhugu looked at her dancer. Her throat was tight.

"You do not have to," said the master. "Nobody will force you. But understand what the choice is. Keep the wax, and you have something lovely for one season. Let it go, and you may have nothing. Or you may have something that will still be dancing in a thousand years. You cannot have both. And once the fire is lit, you cannot turn back."

She did not sleep that night.

In the morning she came to the workshop and began to mix the fine clay.

She put it on with a soft brush and with her fingertips. She covered the smile. She covered the lifted hand, one finger at a time. It felt like burying something. When the last gleam of yellow wax was gone under the grey, she had to go outside for a while.

More clay, and more. She fixed wax rods to make channels for the metal. The lump grew. When it was done, it was an ugly thing: a fat, rough, grey shape, like a clod from a ploughed field, with nothing of the dancer about it.

It dried for many days. Then came the fire.

She held the lump in the tongs herself. She watched the wax run out of the hole at the bottom. It was golden and clear, and it dripped and hissed into a bowl of water. It kept coming. Then it slowed. Then it stopped.

Her dancer was a yellow puddle in a bowl.

She cried then. The master let her.

"Now," he said gently. "While it is hot."

The bronze had been melting in the crucible: copper and tin, glowing like the Sun at evening. Two workers lifted it. They poured. A thin bright stream went down into the dark hole of the ugly lump. It went on until metal brimmed at the top.

Then came the waiting. A night and a day.

Mezhugu sat beside the lump. There was nothing to see. A clod of baked earth. One of the younger boys, tidying up, picked it up by mistake to throw it on the rubbish heap with the broken moulds. She nearly screamed.

"That? I thought it was rubbish. It is so ugly."

"So did I, once," said Mezhugu. She took it back in both arms.

She sat and looked at it. Slowly she began to understand something that she had not understood in all her three years of beating clay.

"Master. When the wax was gone, and before the bronze went in. Where was my dancer?"

"You tell me."

"She was not in the wax. The wax was in the bowl. She was not in the bronze. The bronze was still in the pot. But she was not lost. Every finger. The smile. It was all still there." She touched the rough clay. "It was in here. As a hollow. The empty space kept her shape. This ugly thing held her when nothing else did. All of her, with nothing in it."

"Yes," said the master. "Most people see the wax and see the bronze. They never think of the hour in between, when the whole of the work is carried by an emptiness. The mould is the humblest thing in the workshop. We break it and throw it away. And without it there is nothing."

When the lump was cold, he gave her the mallet.

She tapped. The clay cracked. A piece fell away, and inside, something shone.

She broke the rest with her hands. Out of the rubble came a lifted knee, a swinging skirt and an arm flung wide. They were dark gold and solid. They would not sag in summer, and no mouse could harm them.

Every finger was there. The smile was there.

She sat on the floor among the broken clay with the bronze dancer in her lap.

Before she swept up, she chose one piece of the mould. On its inner side, pressed into the baked clay, was the hollow print of a tiny hand. She kept that piece all her life, on a shelf beside her tools.

"To remember what I had to let go?" the master asked, years later.

"To remember what held it while I did," she said.

## 6.7 The Paper Promise

Seth Vishwas was a merchant in a city of the west. He had to send four hundred gold coins to a cloth dealer in a city of the east, forty days away by road.

Forty days of road meant forty nights of danger. A chest of gold needs guards, guards need paying, and everybody on the road knows what guards are guarding.

"I shall not send gold," said Seth Vishwas. "I shall send Hundi."

Hundi was his daughter. She was thirteen, and she had been named after the thing that she was now to carry. A hundi was a letter. It was written by one banker to another banker whom he trusted, far away. It said, in effect: "Pay the bearer of this letter four hundred gold coins, and I will settle with you later."

The banker of the west wrote it out in a careful hand, and pressed his seal on it. He folded it small.

"So I carry a piece of paper," said Hundi, "and at the other end it turns into gold?"

"The paper turns into nothing," said the banker. "Paper is paper. What you carry is my promise. Banker Bharosa in the east has known me for thirty years. In thirty years I have never failed to make good a letter. So when he sees my hand and my seal, he will pay. He is not paying for the paper. He is paying because of thirty years."

"And what if someone steals the paper?"

The banker smiled. "Come closer." He told her something in her ear, and made her say it back three times. "That is not written anywhere. Bharosa and I arranged it long ago. Without it, the paper is only a promise with no one to keep it."

Hundi travelled with a party of pilgrims. She wore plain clothes. The letter was sewn into the hem of her shawl.

On the twenty-third day, in a pass between dry hills, the bandits came.

There were five of them. They did not hurt anybody, but they were thorough. They took the pilgrims' few coins. They took a brass pot. One of them, with quick fingers, felt along the hem of Hundi's shawl and found something stiff.

He cut the stitches and unfolded the letter. The bandits gathered round.

"It is writing," said the leader, disgusted. He could not read. He turned it upside down. "Sewn up like treasure. And it is a letter to somebody's aunt."

"That is a hundi," said the youngest bandit. He was a boy of about fifteen, thin and sharp-eyed, called Chatur. "I have seen merchants with them. It is as good as gold. Take it to the banker whose name is on it, and he pays out."

The leader's eyes lit up. "How much?"

Chatur could read a little. "Four hundred."

The bandits whooped. They rode off with the letter and left the pilgrims standing in the dust.

Hundi sat down on a rock. The pilgrims tried to comfort her. But she was not crying. She was thinking about what the banker of the west had said. Paper is paper.

She went on to the city of the east with the pilgrims, on foot. It took seventeen more days.

The bandits got there first, of course. They had horses.

They scrubbed their faces and put on stolen coats. The leader marched into the counting house of Banker Bharosa and laid the letter on the table.

"Pay me. Four hundred. As it says."

Bharosa was a small, mild man. He read the letter. He looked at the seal. He looked at the leader's boots.

"This is indeed the hand of my friend in the west," he said pleasantly. "And his seal. A true hundi. Please sit. There is only the small matter of the question."

"What question?"

"Ah. My friend and I have a custom. The true bearer of his letters can always tell me one thing. What was the name of the well where he and I first met, thirty years ago?"

The leader stared. "The... the well. It was a deep well."

"No doubt. And its name?"

"What does it matter? I have the paper! The paper says pay!"

"The paper says that my friend promises," said Bharosa. "A promise is made to a person. He did not promise four hundred coins to a piece of palm leaf. He promised them to the one that he sent. The one that he sent knows the well. You do not. So you are not the one that he sent. And so this is only a leaf with ink on it." He folded his hands. "Shall I call the city guard, so that you can tell them how you came by it?"

The bandits left faster than they had come.

That night, in a ruined rest house outside the walls, they shouted at each other.

"As good as gold, you said!"

"It is as good as gold," said Chatur. "In the right hands."

"Then it is worthless. Burn it."

"Wait," said Chatur. He was turning the letter over and over. "I want to understand this. A gold coin is worth the same in anyone's hand. A thief's or a king's. That is why we steal gold. But this is worth four hundred in that girl's hand, and nothing in ours. The paper is the same. So the worth is not in the paper. Where is it?"

"Who cares?"

"I care. That banker would have handed four hundred gold coins to a girl in a dusty shawl, on the word of a man who lives forty days away. No guards. No chest. He would do that because, in thirty years, that man has never lied to him. That is what the four hundred is made of. It is made of a man keeping his word for thirty years. We cannot steal that. We could steal the paper a hundred times, and we would never have it."

The others had stopped listening. They threw the letter in a corner and went to sleep.

Chatur lay awake.

"We have been bandits for three years," he thought. "And we are thin, and we sleep in ruins. Everything that we take is gone in a week, and nobody on any road would trust us with a cup of water. That merchant's daughter carries nothing and is worth four hundred gold coins. I have been stealing the wrong thing. I did not know that the other thing existed."

Before dawn he took the letter from the corner and slipped away.

He waited by the east gate for six days. On the seventh, a party of pilgrims came in, grey with dust. Among them was a girl in a plain shawl with a torn hem.

He stepped in front of her. She knew him at once and went stiff.

He held out the letter. "This is yours. It is no use to us. And I do not think it ought to be."

Hundi took it slowly. "Why?"

"Because I want to know how to become somebody whose paper is worth something."

She looked at him for a while. Then she said, "Come with me."

Bharosa read the letter a second time. "Before you ask," said Hundi, "it is the Well of the Three Neem Trees."

Bharosa counted out four hundred gold coins without another word. Then he looked at the thin boy standing by the door, twisting his cap.

Hundi told him all of it.

"A bandit," said Bharosa.

"He gave it back, sir. He waited six days at the gate to do it."

"Hm. Boy. Can you ride?"

"Yes, sir."

"Can you read?"

"A little, sir."

"I need a messenger to carry letters between this house and three towns to the north. The pay is small. If one letter goes astray, you are finished, with me and with every banker from here to the sea, for we write to each other. If none goes astray for a year, the pay is better. If none goes astray for ten years, people will begin to say, 'Send it by Chatur.' That is worth more than I can pay you. Do you understand what I am offering?"

"Yes, sir," said Chatur. "The first day of thirty years."

## 6.8 The Song and the Rain

In the hill village of Megh there lived an old singer named Ustad Malhar. When he sang the rain song, people said, the clouds came.

Everybody in Megh had a story about it. The year of the great drought, he sang for three nights, and on the fourth morning it poured. At the headman's daughter's wedding, he sang it by mistake, and they all got soaked.

His youngest pupil was a boy of eleven called Boond. Boond believed in the rain song as he believed in the ground under his feet. He had seen it work himself. Last year, in the month before the monsoon, his teacher had sung at dusk, and before midnight the roof was drumming.

"When I am grown," Boond told his friend Ginni, "I will sing it, and no village that I live in will ever be dry."

Ginni was a weaver's daughter. She kept the count of threads for her mother and liked things to add up.

"How do you know that the song brings the rain?"

"I have seen it! He sings, and it rains."

"Every time?"

"Well. Not every time. Sometimes the clouds are stubborn."

"And does it ever rain when he has not sung?"

"Of course. It is the hills. It rains a great deal."

Ginni wrinkled her nose. "Then I do not see how you know. If it rains often anyway, then some of the time he will have sung just before. It would look the same if the song did nothing at all."

Boond was angry. She was speaking against his teacher. But the question was now in his head, and it would not go out. For three days he was cross with everybody.

On the fourth day he went to Ustad Malhar himself. He was afraid. But the old man had always told him, "Bring me your true notes, even the sour ones."

"Ustad-ji, Ginni says that we do not really know if the song brings the rain. I told her that she was wrong. But I could not tell her why."

He waited for thunder. Instead the old man was quiet for a while, tuning a string.

"When I was your age," he said, "my teacher told me that it brought the rain. And his teacher told him. I have sung it for sixty years. I have never counted. A singer does not count. But you are asking whether it is so. That is a different thing from whether it is beautiful. If you want to know whether it is so, then you must count. I will help you."

So they made a plan, the three of them. It was the month before the monsoon, when rain comes on some days and not on others.

For thirty days, Ginni would decide each morning whether the song would be sung that evening. She would decide by pulling a thread from a bag without looking: red for sing, white for silence. "So that Ustad-ji cannot choose the cloudy days without meaning to," she said. "Anyone can smell rain coming. If he sings only when he feels like singing, perhaps he feels like it when rain is near."

Boond would keep the tally on the wall of the music room, with charcoal. Four boxes. Song and rain. Song and no rain. No song and rain. No song and no rain. "Rain" meant rain before the next sunrise.

"Why four boxes?" asked Boond. "We only care about song and rain."

"No," said Ginni. "That was the mistake before. Everybody remembers the times that he sang and it rained. Nobody remembers the other three boxes. We have to fill all four."

Thirty days. Fifteen red threads and fifteen white.

On the red days Ustad Malhar sang at dusk on the roof, with all his heart. Boond sat beside him with the drone and willed the clouds to come.

On the thirtieth evening, the three of them stood before the wall.

Song and rain: four marks. Song and no rain: eleven.

No song and rain: four marks. No song and no rain: eleven.

Boond read it again and again. The two halves of the wall were the same. Four rains in fifteen days with the song. Four rains in fifteen days without it.

"It makes no difference," he whispered. His eyes were hot. "Ustad-ji, it makes no difference at all."

He could not look at his teacher. He had done this. He had taken the old man's sixty years and turned them into charcoal marks.

Ustad Malhar looked at the wall for a long time.

"Well," he said. His voice was a little rough. "There it is. Thank you, both of you."

"You are not angry?"

"I am a little sad. I should have liked to bring the rain. But would you rather that I went on telling farmers in a drought, 'Do not dig a new well, I shall sing'? If it is not so, I need to know that it is not so. More than anybody." He touched the wall. "It rained on four of my fifteen. I would have remembered those four for the rest of my life. And I would have forgotten the eleven."

Boond wiped his nose. "But then what is the song for?"

It was Ginni who answered, slowly. She was looking at her own notes. She was a thorough girl, and she had written down more than she had been asked to.

"I wrote other things each evening. Just what I saw. Look. On the song nights, people came out on their roofs. I counted how many. And I wrote what they did afterwards."

She read it out.

"Day three. Song. The potter's baby, who screams every evening, was quiet from the first note. Day eight. Song. The two old brothers by the well, who do not speak, both sat on the same wall to listen. Day fourteen. Song. Nobody in the lane shouted at anybody until morning. I checked the no-song nights too. On eleven of the fifteen there was shouting in the lane before bedtime. On song nights, there was shouting on two."

She looked up. "Eleven out of fifteen, against two out of fifteen. That is not the same on both sides of the wall. That is a real difference."

Ustad Malhar had gone very still.

"And day twenty-six," Ginni went on. "Song. The old brothers by the well. When it finished, the elder one said, 'Our mother used to hum that.' And the younger one said, 'She did.' They have not spoken for nine years. The next morning they were mending the well rope together."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"So the song does not move the clouds," said Boond.

"No," said the old man.

"But it moves people. Every time. We counted it."

"It seems," said Ustad Malhar, and now he was smiling, with wet eyes, "that for sixty years I have been proud of a power that I did not have. And I never noticed the one that I did."

He sang that evening, although the thread had not said so. The experiment was over. The whole lane came out on the roofs. It did not rain.

Nobody minded.

Boond did grow up to be a singer. When people asked him, "Is it true that your teacher's song could bring the rain?", he always gave the same answer.

"No. We tested it. But come and sit down, and I will show you what it can do."

## 6.9 The Slow Drip

The caravan had been walking for nine days across the white country, where the ground is crusted with salt and shines like snow.

There was one well in the middle of it, the Well of Khara. Every caravan filled its skins there for the second half of the crossing. Without that well, nobody could cross at all.

On the ninth evening they reached it. The caravan master lowered the bucket and drew it up. He drank, and he spat.

"Salt."

It had been a year of poor rain. The sweet water in the well had sunk, and the salt of the ground had crept in. It was not as bad as the sea. But it was too salty to drink. A person who drinks salt water grows thirstier with every cup.

They had one day's sweet water left in the skins. It was five days back to the last good well, and five days on to the next.

The grown-ups sat in a circle and talked in low voices. They talked about which camels to kill for the water in their stomachs. They talked about sending the two fastest riders ahead and hoping.

A girl of twelve named Namak sat outside the circle. She was the daughter of the caravan's cook, and she had spent her life beside cooking pots.

"There is plenty of water," she thought. "The well is full. The trouble is only that salt is in it. Water and salt are two things, mixed. If they are two things, perhaps they do different things. And if they do different things, I can use that to pull them apart."

What did she know about salt and water?

She knew this, from a hundred mornings. When her mother boiled salted water for rice and left the pot too long, the water went away, and a white crust was left on the bottom of the pot.

"The water goes. The salt stays. So heat makes them part. Water flies up as steam. Salt cannot fly."

That was half of an idea. The water did leave the salt behind. But it left as steam, and steam vanished into the air. What good was that?

She thought of the lid of the rice pot. When her mother lifted it, the underside was always covered with drops. They ran together and dripped. Once, as a small child, she had licked them.

She remembered now how they had tasted. They had not tasted of salt.

"The steam goes up, and it has no salt in it. It touches the cool lid. It turns back into water. Sweet water. The lid catches what the fire sets free."

She jumped up and ran to her mother's pots.

It took her an hour to build it, and the grown-ups were too busy with their worries to notice. She took the biggest cooking pot and filled it a third full of salt water from the well. In the middle of the pot, standing on a flat stone so that its rim was above the water, she set a small empty bowl.

For a lid, she turned the big brass serving dish upside down over the pot. It was shaped like a shallow cone, so its lowest point now hung right over the small bowl. Last, she poured a little cold well water into the hollow on top of the dish, to keep it cool.

She lit a small fire of dried camel dung under the pot, and she waited.

The water in the pot began to steam. The steam rose and touched the cool brass. Drops formed. They ran down the inside of the cone to its lowest point, hung there and fell.

Plink. Into the small bowl.

Plink.

It was terribly slow. The Moon moved a hand's width across the sky. When she lifted the dish, there was about a cupful in the small bowl.

She tasted it. She shut her eyes.

Then she carried it into the circle of grown-ups, and held it out to the caravan master.

"What is this?"

"Water from the well, Uncle."

He tasted it. He stared at her. "This is sweet. Where did you...?"

"From the well. I took the salt out. Or really, I took the water out, and left the salt behind."

She showed them. The circle of worried faces gathered round one cooking pot. Plink.

"A cup in an hour," said one of the men. "We are forty people and sixty camels. It is a child's game."

"From one pot," said Namak. "How many pots do we have? I counted nineteen, and eleven big dishes and trays. We have dung for the fires. A whole heap of it, left here by every caravan before us. And nobody has to stand over them. They drip while we sleep. And in the daytime the Sun is hot enough here to cook an egg on a stone. Paint the pots with soot, cover them, and the Sun will do the work of the fire for nothing."

The caravan master was a man who could change his mind. "Every pot," he said. "Every tray. Now."

They stayed at the Well of Khara for four days. By the second day, the camp was a strange sight: rows of black pots with shining lids, each one going plink, plink, by day and by night. Children ran from pot to pot, emptying the small bowls into the water skins.

It was slow, and it needed patience. More than one person lifted a lid too soon to look, and so let the steam out and lost an hour. Namak made a rule: "Do not look. Listen. If you hear it drip, it is working."

On the fifth morning every skin was full. Nobody had killed a camel.

As they packed up, Namak's mother began to scrub out the big pots. The bottom of each one was thick with a hard white crust.

"Wait!" said Namak. "Do not throw that out."

"It is only the salt, child. The very thing that nearly killed us."

"It is clean salt. White and dry, and we did no work to make it but boil water. In the towns on the far side, where we are going, salt comes from far away. People there pay for it by the handful."

Her mother stopped scrubbing.

They scraped nineteen pots. They filled four sacks.

In the market on the far side of the white country, the salt sold in a morning. When the caravan master had counted the coins, he counted them again. They came to more than the cost of the food and fodder for the whole crossing.

"The well gave us poison," he said. "This child split it in two. Half of it saved our lives. The other half paid our way." He put the coins in Namak's hands. "The salt is the cook's and her daughter's. That is only fair."

Namak bought two things with her share: a new brass dish for her mother, and for herself, a small pot with a lid that she could carry anywhere.

The next year, on the edge of the Well of Khara, somebody set up a flat stone. Scratched on it was a picture, for those who could not read. It showed a pot, a fire and a dish upside down, with one drop falling.

## 6.10 The Empty Hand

Laghvi was the smallest girl in her lane, and there was a boy who made use of that.

His name was Dhakka. He was two years older and a head taller, and he pushed. In the lane he pushed her into the wall. At the well he pushed her bucket over. He did not seem to hate her. Pushing was just what he did with things that were smaller than he was.

At the edge of the town was a fighting school. An old woman named Guru Shanta taught there. People said that in her youth she had thrown three armed men into a river.

Laghvi walked in one morning and said, "Teach me to fight. I want to knock a boy down."

Guru Shanta looked her over. "I will teach you. First lesson: fall down."

"What?"

"Fall down. On the mat. Now."

Laghvi fell down. It hurt.

"Badly done. You fell like a pot, all at once, on one point. Fall like water. Tuck your chin. Round your back. Let your arm slap the mat to spread the blow. Roll. Again."

She fell forty times that day. And the next day. And the next.

After a month she could fall backwards, sideways and forwards. She could be tipped over in the middle of a step and roll up on to her feet. She was bruised in places that she had not known she had.

"When do I learn to hit?" she asked.

"Second lesson," said Guru Shanta. "Step aside."

The old woman walked straight at her. Laghvi stood her ground and was knocked flat.

"Why did you stand there? I am bigger than you. If you stand in front of a big thing that is moving, it knocks you down. Again. This time, at the last moment, do not be there."

It took another month. She learned to wait, to breathe and to watch the hips of the person coming, not the hands. She learned to turn, just a quarter turn, like a door swinging on its hinge, so that the push met only air.

After six months she said, "Guru-ji, I can fall, and I can get out of the way. But I came to learn to fight. Dhakka still pushes me. I only fall better."

"Does it hurt when he pushes you now?"

Laghvi thought. "No. Not for a long time."

"Are you afraid of him?"

She thought again, and she was surprised. "Not very. He pushes. I roll. I get up. It is a bit boring."

"Then you have learned half of fighting already, and the more important half. Most people fight badly because they are afraid of falling. You are not. Now the third lesson. Push me."

Laghvi pushed the old woman with all her strength.

Guru Shanta turned like a door. As Laghvi's push went past, the old woman laid one hand, quite gently, on her elbow and added a little to it. Just a little, in the same direction that Laghvi was already going.

Laghvi flew across the room.

She rolled, of course, and came up staring.

"I did not throw you," said Guru Shanta. "You threw yourself. All of that force was yours. I only did not stop it. And I gave it a finger's weight more. The harder a person comes, the further they go. A big, strong, angry person carries a great deal of force, and they bring it all with them, as a gift. You need none of your own."

"So the bigger he is..."

"The more he gives you to work with. That is why I taught you to step aside before I taught you anything else. The strongest move is nearly always the one that you do not make. Do not meet force with force. If you do, the bigger force wins, and you are small. Let it pass. Then decide where it goes."

For the rest of the year Laghvi learned that. She learned how to feel, through one touch, which way a body was already falling. She learned how little it took.

At the end of the year Guru Shanta said one more thing. "Now that you can do it, you carry a weight. When you send someone's force past you, you choose where it lands. On a mat or on a stone. In the dust or over a wall. They will not be choosing. You will. So what happens to them is yours to answer for. A person who can throw has a harder duty than a person who can only hit."

Laghvi did not fully understand that until the day at the old tank.

The old tank was a deep stone reservoir at the top of the town, with a low wall round it. It was dry at that season. From the lip of the wall to the stone floor was the height of three men.

Laghvi was sitting on the wall, eating a guava. Dhakka came up the path with two friends.

He had not pushed her for some weeks. It had stopped being fun, since she only rolled and got up. But his friends were watching, and she was sitting right on the edge. Perhaps he only meant to make her jump.

He ran at her with both hands out.

Everything went slow, as it had learned to do. She saw his hips. She saw that he was coming much too fast. She saw, behind her, the drop.

She did not think. A year of mornings did it for her. She slid off the wall to one side and turned a quarter turn. The door swung.

Dhakka's hands met nothing. His own rush carried him forward. His shins hit the low wall, and he began to tip over it, head first, his arms wheeling, towards three men's height of empty air and a floor of stone.

That was the moment. She had done nothing to him. She had only not been there. If he fell, it would be by his own push. Anybody would say so.

"They will not be choosing. You will."

Her hand shot out and closed on his wrist. She dropped her weight low, as she had dropped it ten thousand times on to the mat, and sat down hard behind the wall, with both feet braced against the stone.

His whole weight came on her arm. It felt as if her shoulder was being pulled out. But her weight was low, and the wall took the strain. He hung there, bent over the edge, looking straight down at the stone floor, for one long second.

Then he scrambled back and fell in the dust on the safe side. He sat there, grey in the face and shaking.

His friends had not moved.

Laghvi let go of his wrist. Her arm was trembling. She picked up her guava from the wall, saw that it was dusty, and wiped it on her skirt.

"You should not run at people near an edge," she said. Her voice shook a little.

Dhakka looked at the wall. He looked at her. She was still a head shorter than he was.

"You moved," he said hoarsely. "And then you caught me."

"Yes."

"You could have just... moved."

"Yes."

He was silent for a long time. "Why did you not?"

Laghvi thought about how to say it. "Because I am the one who knew what was going to happen. You did not. So it was mine to look after."

Dhakka did not push anybody after that. It was not fear. It was something that he had seen while he was looking down, and it took him years to put into words.

About a month later, a tall boy came to the door of the fighting school.

"I want to learn," he said gruffly.

Guru Shanta looked him up and down.

"First lesson," she said. "Fall down."

## 6.11 The Town of Opposite Tuesdays

Seedhi was walking to her aunt's village, three valleys away, when she took a wrong path in the mist. By afternoon she was thoroughly lost. She came down a hill into a town that she had never seen.

It was a pretty town with a river on its far side. At the end of the main street, the road split in two. One branch went left, down to a stone bridge. The other went right, into a thick wood.

A woman was sweeping her step.

"Excuse me, Auntie. Which road goes to Peepal village?"

"The right-hand one, through the wood," said the woman cheerfully. "You cannot miss it."

Seedhi thanked her and had gone ten steps when a man leading a donkey called out. "Peepal village? Not that way, child! Through the wood, you will be walking until next week. Take the left, over the bridge."

Seedhi stopped. "But the lady said..."

"Ah. You are a stranger." The man scratched his head. "I had better explain. No, wait. I cannot explain. That is the trouble. Ask at the sweet shop. No, do not ask at the sweet shop. Oh dear." He hurried off, looking unhappy.

Seedhi stood in the street, quite muddled.

A small boy was sitting on a wall and swinging his legs. He looked at her with great interest.

"He was trying to tell you about Tuesdays," he said. "But today he cannot. Or he can. I am not telling you which."

"What about Tuesdays?"

"It is the town's rule. It is very old. On Tuesdays, everybody in this town must say the opposite of what is true. Every question, the opposite answer. On the other six days, everyone tells the plain truth."

"That is the silliest rule that I ever heard."

"Is it?" said the boy. On another day that might have meant yes, and today it might have meant no. He grinned.

Seedhi sat down on the wall. She was tired, and the Sun was getting low. She had to choose a road. A wrong choice meant a night in a wood.

"All right," she said to herself. "Think. Two people told me two different things. The woman said right. The man said left. They cannot both be telling the truth. But wait. They live in the same town. It is the same day for both of them. If today is not Tuesday, both of them tell the truth, and they would agree. If today is Tuesday, both of them say the opposite, and they would still agree! They would both say the wrong road. So why did they not agree?"

She frowned. Then she had it.

"The man said, 'You are a stranger.' He broke the rule on purpose, or he kept it on purpose. He was trying to help, and that got him into a tangle. So one of them answered by the rule, and the other answered to help me. I cannot tell which is which. Those two answers are no use. I must begin again."

She turned to the boy. "It all depends on what day it is. If I know the day, I know whether to believe people or to turn them upside down. So I will ask. Is today Tuesday?"

"No," said the boy at once.

"Good. So it is not Tuesday, and people are telling the..." She stopped.

The boy's grin got wider.

"Wait," said Seedhi slowly. "Suppose that today is not Tuesday. Then you tell the truth, and you say, 'No, it is not Tuesday.' But suppose that today is Tuesday. Then you must say the opposite. The true answer is 'yes', so you say 'no'. You say 'no' on Tuesday. You say 'no' on every other day. You say 'no' on every day there is!"

"Do I?" said the boy, delighted.

"That question is useless. In this town, nobody can ever say, 'Yes, today is Tuesday.' On Tuesday they are not allowed to. On the other days it would not be true." She put her head in her hands. "I cannot find out the day by asking. And without the day, I cannot trust any answer."

She was close to tears. Then she got a little angry instead, and that was more useful.

"Very well. I cannot find out by asking. Asking is not the only way to find out a thing. People's words can be bent by a rule. But not everything in a town is words. What cannot lie?"

She looked up the street, properly this time.

A dog was asleep in the road. That told her nothing. A potter's wheel stood still. A woman was hanging out washing. Further up was the sweet shop that the man had mentioned and then un-mentioned. It had a painted board outside, and Seedhi could read.

The board said: FRESH SWEET BREAD. BAKED ON TUESDAYS ONLY.

"Was that board painted on a Tuesday?" she wondered. "No. A board hangs there all week. If it were a lie, it would be a lie on six days and a truth on one. A rule that says 'speak the opposite on Tuesdays' is about what people say when they are asked. Nobody repaints the signs every week."

She walked up to the shop.

On the counter were trays of the ordinary things: hard biscuits and yesterday's rusks. There was no sweet bread. She sniffed. She could smell old oil, and no baking. The big oven at the back was cold. She put her hand near it to be sure.

"A cold oven cannot say the opposite," said Seedhi. "No sweet bread, no smell of baking, a cold oven. Today is not Tuesday."

She felt the knot in her chest come loose.

"So today people tell the truth. Then the woman with the broom told me the truth, and it is the right-hand road. But the man with the donkey said left..." She thought of his worried face. "He knew that I was a stranger. A stranger who had heard of the rule might turn every answer upside down. He was trying to guess what I would do with his answer, and he tied himself in a knot. The woman just answered."

To be quite sure, she asked the baker one question. It was a question with an answer that she already knew.

"Uncle, is your oven hot?"

"Stone cold, child. I bake tomorrow."

It was true. She had felt it. So today, words in this town matched the world.

"And which is the road to Peepal village?"

"Right-hand road, through the wood. An hour's walk. You will be there before dark if you go now."

She went. She was there before dark.

On the way she thought about the strange town, and something came to her that made her laugh aloud in the middle of the wood.

"On Tuesdays, everybody in that town says the opposite. But everybody in that town knows that it is Tuesday. So they all just turn every answer round in their heads, and they understand each other perfectly. Nobody is fooled at all. It is only a game. Their lying works only because they all agree about it. It rests on a truth that they share."

"The only person that it can hurt is a stranger. Somebody who does not know the rule."

"And even a stranger can get through, if she stops asking people what is true and goes to look at an oven."

## 6.12 The Curd Song

In Dahi's house, curd was set every night, and it was her grandmother who set it.

It was the best curd in the lane. It was thick enough to stand a spoon in and it was never sour. Neighbours came with their little pots to borrow a spoonful as a starter. They went home and made thin, sharp, watery stuff, and came back puzzled.

"It is the song," Grandmother always said. "You must sing the curd song while you stir in the starter. From beginning to end. My mother sang it, and her mother. Without the song, the curd does not set properly."

The curd song was a slow, sweet thing about a calf and a river. Grandmother sang it every evening, stirring a spoonful of the old curd into the pot of warm milk. Then she wrapped the pot in an old shawl and put it in the corner by the stove.

Dahi was eleven, and she had begun to wonder about things.

"Nani, how can a song make curd set? The milk has no ears."

"It has set every night for fifty years, child. Do you want to argue with fifty years?"

Dahi did not want to argue. But she did want to know. Her teacher had said something that week that stuck in her mind: "If many things happen together, and you want to know which one matters, change one thing at a time. Only one. Keep all the rest the same."

"Nani, I do not think that you are wrong," she said carefully. "Your curd is the best. Something that you do makes it so. I only want to find out which thing it is. May I try some small pots beside yours?"

Grandmother sniffed. But she was curious too, though she would not have said so. "Use the little clay cups. Do not waste milk."

Dahi wrote down everything that Grandmother did. It was more than she had noticed before.

One: she warmed the milk and tested it with her little finger. Two: she put in one spoonful of old curd. Three: she stirred slowly while she sang the whole song. Four: she wrapped the pot in the shawl. Five: she left it in the warm corner until morning.

That made five things. Any one of them might be the secret, or none.

The first night, Dahi set two cups. Cup A she did exactly as Grandmother did, song and all. That was her measure. Cup B was the same in every way, but she kept her mouth shut. She stirred as long as the song would have taken, humming it inside her head, but she did not let out a sound.

In the morning, both cups were thick and sweet. She could not tell them apart.

"So the sound of the song does nothing," she wrote. She did not tell Grandmother yet. One night was only one night. She did it three nights running. It was the same each time.

Then she tried the words. Cup A as before. For Cup C she sang, out loud, a rude song about a monkey, to the same slow tune. She sang it softly, so that Grandmother would not hear.

Both set perfectly.

"The words do nothing," she wrote. "So it is not the song at all? But Nani's curd is better than everyone's. And she says it is the song. She is not a fool."

She went on. She changed the shawl. An unwrapped cup came out thinner and took longer, so the warmth mattered. She changed the corner. A cup by the cold door hardly set at all, so warmth mattered a great deal. She changed the finger test. With milk too hot, the cup stayed as milk. Too hot kills it, she noted. With milk too cool, it was thin and slow.

"Warmth. Warmth. Warmth. Three of Nani's five things are about keeping the milk just warm enough. But the neighbours do those too. Everybody wraps their pot. So that is not why Nani's is the best."

Only the stirring was left.

Until now she had always stirred for the full length of the song, even in silence. It had never crossed her mind to change that. The stirring and the song were one thing in her head.

"But they are not one thing," she thought suddenly. "They are two. One is the sound. The other is how long."

That night she set three cups. Cup A: the full song. Cup D: she dropped the starter in, gave it two quick turns with the spoon, as she had seen the neighbours do, and stopped. Cup E: she stirred slowly for twice the length of the song, until her arm ached.

In the morning she lined them up.

Cup A was thick, smooth and sweet. It was Grandmother's curd.

Cup D was set, but badly. There was a thick lump in the middle, where the starter had sat. Round the edges it was thin and watery, with a sharp taste. It was exactly the neighbours' curd.

Cup E had set evenly, but it was loose and soft, and a little sour. The milk had cooled too much while she stirred and stirred.

She tasted all three again. Then she sat back on her heels, and she began to smile.

"Nani! Nani, come and see. I have found it. You were right!"

Grandmother came, wiping her hands.

"It is the song. But not the tune and not the words. It is how long the song is. Look. With two quick stirs, the starter stays in a lump, so the middle sets and the edges do not. If I stir for too long, the milk gets cold before it is wrapped. But if I stir slowly for just as long as the curd song takes, the starter is spread through every drop, and the milk is still warm. The song is the right length. It is a timer. It tells your hand when to stop."

Grandmother looked at the three cups. She tasted D and made a face. "That is what the neighbours make."

"Because you give them the starter, and you tell them to sing. They think that singing is silly, so they do not. They stir twice and wrap it. Nobody ever told them to stir slowly for so long, because nobody knew that that was the part that mattered. It was hidden inside the song."

Grandmother was quiet. Then she said, "My mother could not count past twenty. Her mother could not count at all. There was no water clock in the house. How would you tell a girl, 'Stir for this long, no more, no less'? You would give her a song."

"And the song is slow, so she stirs slowly," said Dahi. "And it is the same every night, so the curd is the same every night. Whoever made that song was very clever. They just did not leave a note to say why."

"So for fifty years I have said that the milk hears the song." Grandmother gave a short laugh. "And for fifty years it was only my arm that was listening."

"But your curd was right all along, Nani. The reason was a bit off. The doing was not."

That week Dahi went round the lane. She did not tell the neighbours that her grandmother had been wrong, for that was not what she had found. She said, "Stir slowly. For as long as it takes to sing this." And she taught them the song about the calf and the river.

Most of them thought that the singing was silly. But they did it, to keep count.

The curd in the lane got a great deal better.

Dahi kept her notebook. On the last page of the curd experiments she wrote something that she went back to many times when she was older and people said to her, "That is just an old custom."

"When old people say, 'Do it this way,' and give a strange reason, there are two mistakes that I can make. One is to believe the reason without testing. The other is to throw away the doing because the reason sounds wrong. Test it first. Change one thing at a time. Sometimes the custom is carrying something true that nobody remembered to explain."
