## 1. Sushruta

Long ago, more than two thousand years back, a teacher named Sushruta lived in the city of Kashi, on the bank of the Ganga. He was a healer. People came to him with broken bones, deep cuts and wounds from battle.

In those days there were no hospitals like ours and no machines. A healer had only his eyes, his hands and what he had learned. So Sushruta looked very closely. He studied the body part by part: the bones, the muscles, the joints, the skin. He believed that a person who wants to heal the body must first know the body well.

He also believed that reading alone is not enough, and doing alone is not enough. A healer needs both. So he gave his students practice before they ever touched a sick person. They learned to cut by cutting gourds and cucumbers. They learned to stitch on cloth and on leather. They practised on bags filled with water, so that their hands would become steady and gentle. Only after long practice were they allowed to treat people.

Sushruta and his students used more than a hundred tools. Many were shaped like the beaks and jaws of birds and animals, because he had watched how those hold and grip. He is remembered for mending noses that had been cut, by using skin from the cheek or forehead. Doctors still study this method.

He asked his students to keep clean, to keep their nails short, to speak softly to the sick and to treat a patient like their own family. He told them to go on learning all their lives.

What he knew was gathered in a large book, the Sushruta Samhita. It describes hundreds of illnesses, plants used as medicine and ways of doing surgery. Later it was carried to other lands and put into other languages.

We know little about Sushruta's own days: what he ate, where he slept, who his family were. What has stayed is his way of working. Look carefully. Practise with your hands until they are sure. Be kind to the person in front of you.

## 2. Ibn Sina

Ibn Sina was born about a thousand years ago near the city of Bukhara, in Central Asia. In Europe people called him Avicenna.

As a boy he was hungry to learn. His father found him teachers, and he learned to count from a man who sold vegetables in the market and knew the Indian way of using numbers. Soon the boy was reading on his own, late into the night, with a lamp beside him.

Not everything came easily. One book, by the Greek thinker Aristotle, he could not understand at all. He read it forty times. He knew the words by heart, and still the meaning stayed hidden. Then one day in the market a bookseller pressed a small, cheap book into his hands. It explained the hard one. Ibn Sina read it and at last he understood. He was so glad that the next day he gave food and money to the poor.

He turned to medicine as a teenager. He read what earlier doctors had written, and he also sat with sick people and watched them closely. When he was about seventeen, the ruler of Bukhara fell ill, and the young man helped to cure him. Asked what reward he wanted, Ibn Sina asked for one thing: to be let into the royal library. There he found books he had never seen.

His grown-up life was not calm. His father died, rulers changed, and there were wars. He moved from city to city. For a time he had to hide, and once he was shut in a fortress. Through all this he kept writing. He worked for rulers and treated patients by day, and wrote at night.

His best-known book is the Canon of Medicine. In it he put in order what was known about the body, illness and medicines, and added what he had seen himself. He wrote that some illnesses pass from person to person, and that the state of the mind affects the body. Students of medicine in Asia and Europe used this book for about six hundred years.

He also wrote on stars, numbers, music and how to think clearly. He died in 1037. To him, a day without learning something new felt like a day not fully lived.

## 3. Florence Nightingale

Florence Nightingale was born in 1820 into a rich English family. She was named after Florence, the city in Italy where she was born. Her father taught her at home: languages, history and mathematics. She loved numbers and kept careful lists of everything.

As a young woman she felt sure that her work in life was to care for the sick. Her parents were shocked. In those days hospitals were dirty and crowded, and nursing was thought to be no work for a daughter of such a family. They said no.

Florence did not shout, and she did not give up. For years she rose before sunrise and quietly read reports about hospitals and health. When the family travelled, she visited hospitals. At last, at thirty-one, she went to Germany for training as a nurse.

In 1854 Britain was at war, far away in Crimea. News came that wounded soldiers were dying in great numbers. Florence went with thirty-eight nurses to an army hospital in Turkey. What she found was terrible. The floors were filthy, there were rats, the water was bad, and there were not enough beds, blankets or bandages. More men were dying of fevers caught in the hospital than of their wounds.

She and the nurses set to work. They scrubbed, washed the sheets, opened windows, and set up a kitchen for proper food. At night Florence walked the long rooms with a lamp, stopping at each bed. The soldiers called her the Lady with the Lamp.

After the war she did something just as important. She had counted everything: who died, when and of what. She turned these numbers into clear pictures and charts, so that the people in power could see at a glance that dirt and bad air were killing soldiers. Rules for hospitals changed.

In 1860 she opened a school in London to train nurses. She wrote a small book, Notes on Nursing, for ordinary homes. She was often ill herself and spent many years working from her bed, writing letters and reports, some of them about health in the villages and towns of India.

She died in 1910, at ninety. She put care and counting together, and both were needed.

## 4. Anandibai Joshi

Anandibai Joshi was born in 1865 in Kalyan, near Mumbai. Her name then was Yamuna. As was the custom, she was married at nine, to a man much older than her, Gopalrao Joshi. He gave her the name Anandi.

Gopalrao was a postal clerk with an unusual idea for those times: he wanted his wife to study. Girls were rarely sent to school then. He taught her himself, to read Marathi, then Sanskrit, then English. He could be a hard teacher, and study was not always pleasant for her. But she learned.

At fourteen Anandi had a baby boy. He lived only ten days. There had been no proper doctor to care for him. Her grief turned into a question: why should mothers and babies die for want of a doctor? Many women would not let a man doctor treat them, and there were no women doctors. She decided to become one.

There was no place in India then where she could do this. A letter from Gopalrao, asking for help, was printed in an American paper. A woman in New Jersey, Theodicia Carpenter, read it and wrote to Anandi. The two became friends through letters.

Many people around Anandi were angry. A woman crossing the sea, alone, to study? Before leaving she stood up in a hall in Serampore, Bengal, and explained in clear words why she was going: India needed women doctors, and she would go and come back as herself.

In 1883, at eighteen, she sailed from Kolkata. The journey took about two months. In America the cold was hard on her, and the food was strange. She kept wearing her sari and kept to her own way of eating. She studied at the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania and wrote her final paper on the care of mothers and babies. In 1886 she became a doctor.

But she had fallen ill with tuberculosis. She came home to a warm welcome and was offered charge of the women's ward at a hospital in Kolhapur. She did not live to work there. She died in 1887, a month before her twenty-second birthday.

Her life was short. After her, the door was open, and other women walked through it.

## 5. Dhyan Chand

Dhyan Singh was born in 1905 in Allahabad. His father was a soldier, so the family moved from place to place and at last settled in Jhansi. The boy left school after class six. As a child he liked wrestling and did not think much about hockey.

At sixteen he joined the army as an ordinary soldier. There he began to play hockey with the other men. An officer, Subedar-Major Bale Tiwari, saw something in the way he moved and began to teach him.

A soldier's day is full of duty, so Dhyan had only the night for practice. There were no lights on the ground. He waited for the moon to rise and then practised alone, hour after hour, pushing the ball, turning, stopping, starting again. His friends began to call him Chand, the moon. The name stayed. He became Dhyan Chand.

He practised small things again and again. He ran with the ball along the rail of a railway track, keeping it on the narrow line. In time the ball seemed to stay close to his stick like a friend. People who watched could not understand how he did it. He said it was simple: practice, and thinking about where everyone on the field was standing.

In 1928 he went with the Indian team to the Olympic Games in Amsterdam. It was a long journey by ship, and the players had little money. India took the gold medal there, and again in Los Angeles in 1932 and in Berlin in 1936, where Dhyan Chand was captain.

On the field he did not play for himself alone. He often passed the ball to a teammate who was better placed. He said hockey is a game of eleven people.

He stayed in the army for more than thirty years and retired as a Major. Afterwards he coached young players. He lived simply in Jhansi and never became rich. He died in 1979.

In India his birthday, 29 August, is kept as National Sports Day. What people remember most is a young soldier on an empty ground at night, practising by the light of the moon.

## 6. Jesse Owens

James Cleveland Owens was born in 1913 in Alabama, in the south of the United States. He was the youngest of ten children. His grandparents had been slaves, and his father farmed land that belonged to someone else. As a small boy he was thin and often sick with a bad chest, and he picked cotton in the fields with his family.

When he was nine the family moved north to the city of Cleveland to look for a better life. On his first day at the new school the teacher asked his name. He said "J.C." in his southern way of speaking. She heard "Jesse" and wrote that down. The name stayed.

At school a sports teacher, Charles Riley, watched him run. Jesse could not come to practice after school, because he worked in the evenings: he carried groceries and worked in a shoe repair shop. So Mr Riley met him in the morning, before school began. Day after day they practised. The teacher taught him to run lightly, as if the ground were hot, and to look ahead, not at the others.

Jesse went on to university. Even there life was not fair to him. Because he was Black, he was not allowed to live in the hostel with white students. When the team travelled, he had to eat in separate places. He worked at small jobs to pay his way, and he kept training.

In 1936 the Olympic Games were held in Berlin, Germany. The rulers of Germany at that time said that some races of people were better than others. Jesse Owens ran and jumped there and received four gold medals. In the long jump a German athlete, Luz Long, talked with him in a friendly way during the contest. Afterwards the two walked around the stadium arm in arm, in front of everyone. They wrote letters to each other for years.

Back home, medals did not bring him easy days. He still had to enter some buildings by the back door, and for years he struggled to find steady work. Later he spent much of his time working with young people.

He died in 1980. He often said that the friendship with Luz Long was worth more to him than his medals.

## 7. Milkha Singh

Milkha Singh was born around 1929 in a village called Govindpura, which is now in Pakistan. His family were farmers. As a boy he walked a long way to school, barefoot, over sand that burned in summer.

In 1947 the country was divided, and there was terrible violence. Milkha lost his parents and several brothers and sisters. He was a teenager. He reached Delhi by train, alone, with nothing. For a time he lived in a camp for refugees and did whatever work he could find.

He tried to join the army and was turned away three times. On the fourth try, in 1951, he got in. There, new soldiers were asked to run a long race across open country. Those who did well were promised an extra glass of milk each day. Milkha ran. He did well enough to be picked for training, and for the first time someone told him he could be a runner. He did not even know what "400 metres" meant. He asked, and he learned.

Then he worked, harder than his body sometimes allowed. He trained after his duties, late in the day. He ran on hills, on river sand and beside moving trains. Some days he ran until he fell. When he met runners from other countries, he asked them how they trained, wrote it down and tried it.

In 1958 he won gold medals at the Asian Games and the Commonwealth Games. In 1960, at the Olympic Games in Rome, he ran the 400 metres final. Partway through he slowed a little, afraid he had started too fast. He came fourth, by a tiny gap. That moment hurt him all his life, and he spoke of it openly. His time stayed the Indian record for about forty years.

The same year he was invited to race in Pakistan. He did not want to go back to the land where he had lost his family. He went in the end, and ran so well that the leader of Pakistan called him the Flying Sikh.

Later he looked after sport in Punjab and helped young athletes. He gave away his medals to the nation. He died in 2021. He always said that his running came from hard work and a strong will.

## 8. Wilma Rudolph

Wilma Rudolph was born in 1940 in Tennessee, in the United States. She was the twentieth of twenty-two children. Her father carried bags at a railway station, and her mother cleaned other people's houses. Wilma was born too early and was very small.

She was sick again and again as a little child. Then, at about five, she caught polio. Her left leg became weak and twisted. A doctor said she might never walk properly.

Her mother did not accept this. The hospital in their town did not treat Black people, so twice a week mother and daughter rode a bus for about eighty kilometres to a hospital in the city of Nashville. They did this for two years. At home the doctors' exercises had to be done every day. Her mother taught the older children how to rub and move Wilma's leg, and they took turns, several times a day.

Wilma wore a metal brace on her leg. Other children ran and played while she sat and watched. She was taught at home until she was seven. She hated the brace, and when no one was looking she took it off and tried to walk. Slowly the leg grew stronger. By the time she was twelve she walked without the brace and without a special shoe.

Then she wanted to do everything she had missed. She played basketball with her brothers in the yard, and later for her school. A running coach, Ed Temple, saw her and invited her to train with his college runners in the summer. The training was hard: long runs across the hills, day after day. At sixteen she went to her first Olympic Games and came home with a bronze medal.

In 1960, at the Olympic Games in Rome, she ran in three races and received a gold medal in each one.

Her home town wanted to hold a parade for her. At that time Black people and white people there were kept apart. Wilma said she would come only if everyone could take part together. So they did, for the first time in that town.

She finished college, became a teacher and a coach, and worked with children for the rest of her life. She died in 1994.

## 9. Pele

Edson Arantes do Nascimento was born in 1940 in a small town in Brazil. His parents named him after Thomas Edison, the inventor. The family was poor. His father had been a footballer, but a knee injury ended his playing, and money was always short.

Edson grew up in the town of Bauru. There was no money for a football. So he and his friends made one: a sock stuffed with newspaper and rags, tied with string. Sometimes they kicked a grapefruit. They played barefoot in the street for hours.

To help at home he shined shoes at the railway station. His father taught him football in the yard: how to use both feet, how to head the ball, how to keep his balance. His father told him that skill is a gift, but it becomes something only with practice.

At school, other boys started calling him Pele. Nobody knows quite why. He did not like the name at first and even got into a fight over it. But it stayed.

When he was fifteen, a coach took him by train to the big club Santos, far from home. He was small and thin, and he was homesick. One night he packed his bag to run away, and a worker at the club sent him back to bed. He stayed, trained with grown men and learned.

At seventeen he was picked for Brazil for the World Cup of 1958 in Sweden. He had a hurt knee and waited through the first games. Then he played, and Brazil took the cup. When the last game ended, the boy cried on the shoulder of the goalkeeper.

Not everything after that went well. In the World Cup of 1962 he was injured early. In 1966 other players kicked him so hard and so often that he limped off the field, and he said he would never play in a World Cup again. He changed his mind, trained hard and played in 1970, when Brazil took the cup again.

Over his life he scored more than a thousand goals. He liked to say that football is a game for a team, and that no one plays alone. People around the world called football "the beautiful game" after him.

He died in 2022.

## 10. P.T. Usha

P.T. Usha was born in 1964 in a village in Kerala and grew up in the small town of Payyoli, near the sea. Her family did not have much money. As a little girl she was often ill and was not strong.

But she loved to run. At school, when she was in class four, she ran against the school's fastest girl, who was three years older, and reached the line first. Her teachers saw it and remembered.

When Usha was twelve, the government of Kerala opened a sports school for girls in Kannur. Forty girls were chosen, and she was one. She received 250 rupees a month for food and other needs. It meant living away from her home and family while she was still a child.

There a coach, O.M. Nambiar, noticed her light, quick steps. He trained her for many years. Their day began before sunrise. She ran on the beach at Payyoli, where the soft sand pulls at the feet and makes the legs strong. She ran up slopes. She ran in the rain. Proper running tracks and good shoes were hard to find, and so was the right food for an athlete. She trained with what she had.

At sixteen she went to the Olympic Games in Moscow. She did not get far, but she saw how the best in the world prepared, and she came back and worked more.

In 1984, at the Olympic Games in Los Angeles, she ran the 400 metres hurdles, a race she had started learning only a short while before. In the final she missed the bronze medal by one hundredth of a second, less than the blink of an eye. She cried. Then she went back to training.

The next year, at the Asian Championships in Jakarta, she received five gold medals. In 1986, at the Asian Games in Seoul, she received four. People called her the Payyoli Express.

She stopped running, became a mother, and then came back to the track and ran again for India.

Later she opened a school of athletics in Kerala. There, girls from villages and small towns live, study and train, with the tracks, food and care that she herself did not have as a child.

## 11. Sachin Tendulkar

Sachin Tendulkar was born in 1973 in Mumbai. His father was a writer and teacher of Marathi. Sachin was the youngest in the family, a restless boy, always climbing trees and getting into small fights.

His elder brother Ajit saw how he swung a bat and, when Sachin was eleven, took him to a coach, Ramakant Achrekar, at Shivaji Park. On the first day Sachin was nervous and played badly. Ajit asked the coach to watch once more from behind a tree, so that the boy would not know. This time he played freely, and the coach took him in.

From then on, his days were cricket. To be near the ground, he went to live with his aunt and uncle. He practised in the morning, played a match in the day and practised again in the evening. He had only one set of cricket clothes. He washed it, hung it up and wore it again, sometimes still damp.

When he was tired at the end of the day, the coach would put a one-rupee coin on top of the stumps. Any bowler who got Sachin out could take the coin. If nobody got him out, the coin was his. He kept thirteen of those coins all his life.

He had first wanted to be a fast bowler. A famous bowler told him plainly that batting suited him better. He listened.

At sixteen he played for India for the first time, in Pakistan. A fast ball hit him on the nose and it bled. He would not leave the field. He wiped his face and went on batting.

He played for India for twenty-four years. It was not all easy. He had injuries to his back, his toe and his elbow. At one time he could not even lift a bat, and he feared he would never play again. He was once made captain and it did not go well. Each time he went back to the nets and practised.

His father told him that being a good person matters more than being a good cricketer, because cricket lasts some years and the person lasts a lifetime. Sachin often repeats this.

He stopped playing in 2013. In his last speech he thanked, one by one, the people who had helped him: his parents, his brother, his coach, his family.

## 12. Mary Kom

Mary Kom was born in a village called Kangathei in Manipur, in the north-east of India. Her parents worked in other people's fields. Mary was the eldest child. Before and after school she helped on the land, cut wood, carried water and looked after her younger sister and brother.

She loved sport of every kind. At school she ran and threw the javelin. In 1998 a boxer from Manipur, Dingko Singh, came home with a gold medal from the Asian Games, and the whole state was full of joy. Mary, a teenager, began to think about boxing.

She left her village for the city of Imphal and went to a coach to ask to be taught. She was small and thin. He was not sure. She waited until everyone else had gone and asked again. He let her join. She trained late into the evening, after the others left. Her gloves and shoes were old and cheap.

She did not tell her father. In those days almost no girls boxed, and she knew he would worry. In 2000 she won a state contest, and her picture was printed in the newspaper. That is how he found out. He was angry and afraid: she might be hurt, and who would marry a girl who boxed? Mary did not fight with him. She explained, and she kept training. In time he became one of her strongest supporters.

She became world champion six times. She married Onler, a footballer, who stood by her work. In 2007 she had twin sons. Many people said a mother could not come back to boxing. She trained again, slowly at first, and became world champion once more.

When women's boxing entered the Olympic Games in 2012, there was no class for her own weight. She had to box against bigger, heavier women. She received a bronze medal in London.

School had been hard to fit in with sport, and she had once failed an important exam. She went back, finished school through open schooling and later finished college too.

In Imphal she runs a place where young people from poor families learn boxing without paying. She lives there with her family, and she still trains.
